This article by Nancy Flores originally appeared in the May 16, 2026 edition of Contralínea, Mexico's leading investigative journalism magazine.

One of the most important challenges in Mexican politics is preventing organized crime, particularly drug trafficking, from infiltrating the branches of government and all three levels of government through candidacies or party financing. In this regard, Morena's new president explains to Contralínea that the ruling party is strengthening its mechanisms to shut the door on criminals, with the cooperation of the Security Cabinet, the Financial Intelligence Unit (UIF), and the INE's oversight. Ariadna Montiel recalls that the foundational struggle of the National Regeneration Movement, led by AMLO, was to combat corruption and also the privileges enjoyed at public expense, and that the principles of the political institute she now leads are precisely "don't lie, don't steal, and don't betray the people," which will govern her leadership. Another challenge the former Secretary of Welfare will emphasize involves the tensions between Mexico and the United States; she therefore announces a fierce defense of the homeland and sovereignty, beginning with the march on Saturday, May 16 in Chihuahua. In the interview, she also describes as unfounded the criticisms against her alleging she would take the Welfare program registries to Morena: that is a serious crime punishable by prison, she reminds.

The federal Security Cabinet — which includes civilian and military intelligence agencies — the Financial Intelligence Unit, and the oversight bodies of the National Electoral Institute (INE) will be strategic in strengthening the mechanisms available to the National Regeneration Movement party (Morena) to prevent organized crime infiltration, particularly drug trafficking, in candidacies and campaign financing, according to the party's new president, Ariadna Montiel Reyes.

Interviewed by Contralínea at the ruling party's offices, the leader emphasizes that the vetting process for aspiring candidates for elected office will be exhaustive. "All candidates, all, all, all of them, will undergo analysis by security agencies. Both we as Morena [will conduct a rigorous examination], and I understand the electoral authority must also proceed to do so to detect, where applicable, any situation we might not catch from the evaluation we conduct."

Montiel Reyes states that "combating organized crime is the task of the country's security forces, of the Security Cabinet as a whole, and we will ask for their support to pass through the filter of reviewing government information, that nobody is linked to criminal activity, of any kind."

Due to this type of vulnerability, two cases have recently put Morena in the spotlight: the U.S. Department of Justice accusation against Sinaloa's governor on leave, Rubén Rocha Moya, and nine other current or former officials from that state, alleging ties to the Chapitos faction of the Pacific Cartel; and the case of Hernán Bermúdez Requena, former Secretary of Public Security of Tabasco — appointed to that position by Senator Adán Augusto López Hernández when he was governor — as Bermúdez Requena allegedly led La Barredora, a criminal group dedicated to extortion and fuel trafficking in the state.

And although in the first case, Mexico's government has so far insisted that the United States has not handed over evidence, it is known that the Department of Justice has testimony from the Chapitos themselves — brothers Ovidio and Joaquín Guzmán López, sons of El Chapo Guzmán, who seek to become protected witnesses of the U.S. government to obtain procedural benefits — in addition to information provided by Gerardo Mérida Sánchez, former Secretary of Public Security of Sinaloa under Rocha Moya's government, who voluntarily surrendered to U.S. authorities on May 15 seeking a deal, and was detained and transferred to New York for prosecution.

Notwithstanding these two cases, the challenge of preventing organized crime, and particularly drug trafficking, from infiltrating the branches of government and all three levels of government belongs to all of Mexican politics, not just the ruling party. An example is what occurred under governments of other parties: under PAN's Felipe Calderón, the federal Secretary of Public Security (from December 2006 to November 2012), Genaro García Luna, allied himself with the Sinaloa Cartel while supposedly leading the fight against drug trafficking. He was sentenced in October 2024 in New York to 38 years in prison.

Other similar cases include: PAN's Francisco Javier García Cabeza de Vaca, former governor of Tamaulipas (2016-2022), who faces federal charges for organized crime, money laundering, and tax fraud; as well as PRI members: Mario Villanueva Madrid, former governor of Quintana Roo (1993-1999), sentenced in Mexico and the United States for collaborating with the Juárez Cartel; Tomás Yarrington, former governor of Tamaulipas (1999-2004), sentenced in the United States to 9 years in prison for accepting bribes from the Gulf Cartel; Eugenio Hernández, former governor of Tamaulipas (2005-2010), accused of money laundering and ties to crime; Roberto Sandoval, former governor of Nayarit (2011-2017), sanctioned by the U.S. Treasury Department and prosecuted in Mexico for operations with illicit funds; and Édgar Veytia, former attorney general of Nayarit, sentenced in a U.S. court to 20 years in prison.

Added to this is that, during the PRI era in government, Miguel Nazar Haro — who served as director of the political police, the Federal Security Directorate (DFS) — was identified as protecting the founders of the now-defunct Guadalajara Cartel, and for his participation in crimes linked to the Dirty War period. The DFS operated between 1947 and 1985, and in the 1970s-1980s was considered a "bridge" between the government and organized crime. In fact, the leaders of the Guadalajara Cartel — Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo (the "Boss of Bosses"), Rafael Caro Quintero, Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo (Don Neto), and Amado Carrillo Fuentes (the "Lord of the Skies") — operated as "agents" of that institution, and held the famous DFS credentials.

Asked about the fight against corruption led by President López Obrador and the campaigns labeling them narco-president, narco-candidate, narco-party, and narco-government, pushed by the political and media right against Morena and its representatives in power — now exploiting the cases the U.S. justice system is building against current and former Sinaloa officials — Ariadna Montiel Reyes considers that the party's principles are to not lie, not steal, and not betray the people.

"The first thing we must be very clear about is that the fight against corruption was the fundamental axis of our movement's struggle throughout all the years López Obrador led it. And that corruption was what had impoverished our country. And the privileges ended, the lavish spending ended, the government bonuses ended since President López Obrador came to power.

"Having worked with both, with the president [Claudia Sheinbaum] a very important austerity policy has also been implemented. From the day we arrived at Sedesol to the day I handed over the Secretariat of Welfare, we reduced the bureaucratic structure operating the Secretariat by half and prioritized fieldwork and closeness with the people. And the president contributed an important part, but the president perhaps doesn't say it as much, but she is also making an important austerity effort, a more efficient budget operation. And tremendous progress has been made.

"So the Fourth Transformation has achieved that. And this year, when welfare programs have a social investment of 1 trillion pesos, we say: 'and where was the trillion pesos before?' In the pockets of a few, in the businesses of a few. That trillion that exists today, where was that trillion in the past? So, we must vindicate the fight against corruption not as a bureaucratic procedure, but as an ethical principle of our Movement. And we are going to do the work of raising awareness with everyone that we must continue on this path, that this is the greatest strength that has allowed us to help people and grow our actions. And we must start with ourselves."

Montiel Reyes adds that, in its essence, Morena has those three principles of not stealing, not lying, and not betraying the people of Mexico, and therefore the party she now leads must always walk that path. "And clearly we have stated that governments born from Morena must work with honesty and that those who represent us must also be honest people; that first and foremost is an ethical-political condition. But, moreover, we must also say that the narrative the opposition is building has to do with the fact that this is our greatest strength, and that is where we have been able to consolidate the Fourth Transformation. And so they want to attack us at what matters most to us."

In that context, Morena's president criticizes the leaders of the opposition parties. "Alito Moreno [Alejandro Moreno, PRI leader] comes to question us. And it's not me saying it, the people say it and the polls show it: the PRI is the political party with the most rejection in the country, and its current national leader is the worst-rated public figure today. So, we also have to put in proper perspective who is saying it; and we maintain our body and method of informing the people to tell them the truth."

Regarding the political right and the National Action Party (PAN), Montiel Reyes observes that they have formed an alliance with some media outlets to maintain a permanent attack on the National Regeneration Movement, but warns that the people don't believe them, because they now receive the support and development works they didn't before. "So people say: 'well, I have my resources, nobody has stolen anything from me, on the contrary, the Fourth Transformation project is supporting me, considering me. And there are more and more actions benefiting the people.' So that is countered with good actions by the governments, by the president's government, by our fellow governors."

The national leader also acknowledges the work of the Movement's legislators, as they have approved constitutional reforms benefiting the people, and notes that the party's commitment is to create the conditions to keep the path of honesty, which is why they will also seek to reclaim the concept of courageous honesty.

Montiel Reyes observes that the Movement's principles have made it possible to reduce poverty in the country, as that is achieved only through investment and budget. "But we must be very brave in going out to the streets, informing, raising awareness, and not backing down; and as I also said the day I was elected Morena's president: stand tall because we have maintained loyalty to the project. And whoever feels they don't belong here, let them know: this is how things are in Morena and we will walk this path. It is the path that legitimately brought us to power, and we want to be in power not for power's sake, but to continue supporting the people and building welfare for the people."

Asked whether there will be more filters to prevent corruption, she responds: "Well, the survey filter is very important. It's not just a survey that only asks if people know you. The survey measures recognition, but it also asks about your attributes, your honesty, your closeness to the people, your knowledge of the territory, which involves knowing the people's problems and also whether the people are willing for you to represent them. So these are much broader values than what we know, what gets published, about whether this person or that one is more famous. Because otherwise we could nominate many famous people, and that's not what this is about: politics is something else. And the filters have to do with evaluating honesty and all these attributes and principles that the people see in each of the candidates."

So, in due time, these are part of the elements that will be taken into account to decide candidacies, and everyone must walk — as my mother says — the straight line they taught us since childhood. And if along the way someone gets confused, that is where we also have to be. And the colleagues who come to join from other political forces or other political backgrounds must be valued and accepted. Morena must keep its doors open, but we also have the responsibility to guide that construction.

Asked about accepting people from outside, she responds: "Well, we live in the work of convincing others about our Movement. So we cannot close Morena's doors, but we have to be a guide for the Movement."

[...] That is why I am so convinced and I will put my greatest efforts into territorial work, which is what I have done all my life and know how to do, because I believe that is also where we face reality, and it makes us more aware.

On the topic of transparency and accountability for party finances, with Morena receiving the largest share of the more than 7 billion peso budget for political parties, Montiel Reyes responds: "Morena has contributed to transparency in the use of resources, which is not just transparency for transparency's sake, but also republican austerity, and that the resources allocated to the political party be applied fundamentally to the territory, to fieldwork. And to comply with all the electoral regulations that are increasingly complex, especially regarding oversight. And that's what it's about: ensuring that the resources we political parties use are of institutional origin from the INE or what the law allows. And in light of this, also be transparent."

She adds that all information must be available on portals and comply with data protection laws, noting this is especially important given the significant sums involved. She mentions that in the coming days they will establish a full review of these matters, and expresses hope that by 2027 they can advance Plan C to continue democratizing the country and making democracy cost the nation less.

Asked about the tensions Mexico faces from U.S. government actions and the need to defend sovereignty, Montiel Reyes states it will be central to her action as Morena's president: "I believe it is the most important issue at this juncture. It has to do not only with our Movement, which is very important, it has to do with the entire nation. It has to do with the depths of who we are as Mexicans. And we have experienced this situation where the possibility of foreign interference has been increasing, but the gravest part is that it has been promoted by Mexicans, especially by the opposition, by the right, which calls for intervention under a pretext that I believe is just that — a pretext — which is 'security.' While it is certainly a very important issue, so important that the president has put her full effort into it throughout her government, the opposition is clearly seeking help from abroad."

She continues: "Now we are seeing the visit of the president of the Community of Madrid [Isabel Díaz Ayuso] with positions that disrespect our country, our sovereignty. So what the opposition is doing is seeking abroad what it has not been able to build within. They have always been far from the people. During the neoliberal period they could sustain themselves because they did it by force, with electoral fraud, buying people's conscience, deceiving people. Today it is not so because our movement has made a very important contribution in awareness and information. And today the people are informed, they know they have rights, they defend their rights."

"So they are distant from the people and have no possibility of advancing electorally, and that is why they seek the help of foreign countries. And this is a juncture in which we, as a Movement, must give our full support to our president and to the homeland, because while we represent a national project, our fundamental objective is for welfare to reach the people."

On whether there would be mass mobilizations, she responds that they are monitoring the situation, but if necessary they will call on the people to organize. She highlights the case of Chihuahua, where the governor intervened through a U.S. agency on security matters and has refused to appear before the Chihuahua Congress or the Senate to explain herself. "There is significant anger among the people and we will provide a path so that our struggle, which is always peaceful, involves the people. That is fundamental, that the people make the demand their own. And we see the outrage over Ayuso's visit. And we will always be giving our position."
