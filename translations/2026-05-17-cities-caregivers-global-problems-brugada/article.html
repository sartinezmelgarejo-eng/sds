This article by Darren García originally appeared in the May 17, 2026 edition of Revista Contralínea, Mexico's leading investigative journalism outlet.

Local governments in every nation, equivalent to municipalities in Mexico, are the closest to the citizenry, and therefore have the greatest capacity to transform daily life. It is from that level that we must seek to respond to the world's great problems, said Mexico City head of government Clara Brugada Molina.

"We are the ones who face the great urban problems every day. We are the ones who turn on the lights in communities and cities; the ones who sweep the streets; the ones who open schools and health centers. We are the ones who can transform the life of the world from below. So let us build territories of peace […], let us be the pacifist force of this world."

Among the problems affecting major cities worldwide are the climate crisis, scarce access to housing, social inequalities, and urban exclusion. She proposed a shift toward what she called "caregiver cities," which must have the capacity to provide protection to women, children, and all people who have historically and systematically suffered exclusion and marginalization, along with promoting territories of peace in the face of the world's grave conflicts.

Speaking at the World Urban Forum (WUF) held in Baku, Azerbaijan, the capital's leader argued that guaranteeing dignified and adequate housing is the pillar of a broader agenda for territorial transformation. "We need this open and inclusive multilateralism, driven by local governments, by cities, to articulate a new vision and urban action oriented toward social, economic, and environmental transformation," she said.

During her participation in the Women's Assembly, Brugada Molina championed the work of women in caregiving, millions of whom have carried these tasks on their shoulders throughout history while remaining invisible.

Later, she called for the creation of a transformative strategy with a gender focus "for adequate housing for all women and girls," within the framework of what she called "feminist urbanism," and pointed to Mexico's capital as an example, where it has been taken into account in the creation of the so-called Utopías (Units for Transformation and Organization for Inclusion and Social Harmony).

These spaces began in Iztapalapa during her years leading that borough, where 12 were built by the end of her term. As head of government of the nation's capital, Brugada Molina identified reaching 100 Utopías across all of Mexico City as one of her key commitments. These centers bring together sports and cultural facilities alongside various caregiving services and courses on diverse aspects of life. So far, two have been inaugurated: Mixiuhca and Ceylán, in Iztacalco and Azcapotzalco, respectively.

"The urban future of the world cannot be built without women," she added, as it represents a radical shift in the paradigm of city design, placing women at the center of life—their journeys, their caregiving time, and the need to guarantee safe spaces.

She criticized the fact that cities were built with the installation of airports, markets, and major works, but forgot the adequate public infrastructure to support humanity's caregiving needs—"fundamental for sustaining life in common and for freeing women from the modern slavery of unpaid domestic work."

"The home and housing constitute the first space where rights are experienced, but also where the first expressions of inequality and violence often appear." In Mexico City alone, 80 percent of sexual assaults occur in homes, she warned.

Also attending the forum were Mexico City's Secretary of Housing, Inti Muñoz; the capital's Coordinator of International Affairs, Rocío Lobera; the federal Secretary of Agrarian, Territorial and Urban Development, Edna Vega Rangel; Mexico's ambassador to Azerbaijan, Victoria Romero Caballero; and other officials from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.
