This article by Aníbal García Fernández originally appeared in the June 6, 2026 edition of Contralínea, an independent Mexican investigative magazine.

Test

PAN members Felipe Calderón, Vicente Fox, María Eugenia Campos, Margarita Zavala, Santiago Taboada, Marko Cortés, Alessandra Rojo, and Mariana Gómez are actively taking part in strategies to destabilize Mexico economically, politically, and socially, and to undermine the government of President Sheinbaum. For this purpose and others, they maintain extensive ties to the United States government, and they are also connected to international organizations, businesspeople, and far-right politicians such as Isabel Díaz and Cayetana Álvarez.

The National Action Party (PAN) stands out not for any democratic character, but for its deep authoritarianism, its ties to foreign interests, and the corruption that accompanies it. Evidence of this is the government of Felipe Calderón, which left the country mired in the worst wave of violence; whose Secretary of Public Security was Genaro García Luna, convicted in the United States for ties to drug trafficking; and whose current party president, Jorge Romero, is allegedly linked to the so-called real estate cartel.

This party also has ties to the most rancid elements of the international right: Isabel Díaz Ayuso, Iván Duque, Alejandro Chafuen, Brazilian judge Sergio Moro, Gerhard Wahlers and Hans Blomeier (both of the conservative Konrad Adenauer foundation), and Roberto Salinas de León of Atlas Network Latin America. Its deepest connections are with conservative and right-wing organizations that on other occasions have attacked progressive governments—as happened against Venezuela, Cuba, Bolivia, and Honduras—and that now intend to replicate this in Mexico.

The International Foundation for Liberty (FIL), founded in 2002 by writer Mario Vargas Llosa, brings together the Spanish-American right and even figures from other regions. According to its own website, its pillars are the establishment of foundations such as democracy, liberty, and prosperity.

However, it tends to gather those who go against democratic processes. For example, former Mexican president Felipe Calderón was invited in 2023 to the International Forum "Ibero-America: Challenges and Opportunities." There he shared the stage with former Chilean president Sebastián Piñera, linked to the so-called Chacarilla Boys, a group that in the 1970s voiced its opposition to the government of Salvador Allende and, moreover, supported the coup d'état of Augusto Pinochet.

Also present was former Bolivian president Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga, who was a political operator in that Andean country's 2019 coup d'état, and who has proven ties to the US that destabilized the government of Evo Morales.

But a glance at the event reveals still more "champions of liberty"—for example, former Brazilian judge Sergio Moro, famous for building judicial corruption cases against former president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva without any evidence. Moro was a student of the Department of Justice, by way of the United States Embassy in Brazil. That judge is responsible for the breakdown of Brazilian democracy, since through lawfare he prevented Lula da Silva from participating in the elections.

With that judicial process, the rise of Jair Bolsonaro was cemented. His son, congressman Eduardo Bolsonaro, is one of the guests at the conservative conventions of the Conservative Political Action Conference (CPAC), where he shares the stage with Javier Milei, Agustín Laje, Richard Grenell, Tulsi Gabbard, and Henry Kronfle, among others.

At the FIL were Roberto Salinas León, Rocío Guijarro, and Alejandro Chafuen. In Mexico, Salinas de León is better known for his family connection to Ricardo Salinas Pliego, but what unites Salinas León and Chafuen is none other than the Atlas Network.

On the Foundation's board of trustees is Chafuen, of Argentine nationality, and, among others, Roberto Salinas León. On the Steering Committee are Cayetana Álvarez and Álvaro Vargas for Mexico; and Bertha Pantoja and Óscar Ortiz—a politician who operated against the government of Evo Morales—for Bolivia.

Among the linked Mexican businesspeople, one notable figure is Alejandro Ramírez, director of Cinépolis, a member of the boards of directors of BBVA, Grupo Alfa, and the Sundance Institute, and who was named a Young Leader by the World Economic Forum in 2005.

The connection extends even further into Mexican opposition groups against the governments of the Fourth Transformation, since Enrique Krauze appears as a member of the Academic Council. Among the entities affiliated with the Foundation are Caminos de Libertad, México Business Forum, and the Ágora Institute of Strategic Thought, located in Mexico; the Cato Institute, a US think tank opposed to progressive governments; the Friedrich Naumann Foundation, the Liberal Network of Latin America (RELIAL), and the Interamerican Institute for Democracy.

Finally, on the Council of Presidents—the crown jewel of conservatism: Felipe Calderón and Vicente Fox for Mexico; for Venezuela, María Corina Machado and Edmundo González; Mariano Rajoy and José María Aznar for Spain; and also former presidents Mauricio Macri—who saddled Argentina with debt to the International Monetary Fund—and Iván Duque, former president of Colombia.

In 2021, Casa América hosted another gathering point for the transnational right, holding the 14th Atlantic Forum, "Ibero-America: Democracy and Liberty in Harsh Times." There we again find Enrique Krauze, Roberto Salinas, and Iván Duque, joined by the then right-wing presidents Sebastián Piñera, Guillermo Lasso of Ecuador, and Luis Lacalle of Uruguay, as well as Leopoldo López, a National Coordinating member of Voluntad Popular, and Mauricio Macri, among others.

The event was closed by Isabel Díaz Ayuso, a Spaniard who was invited to Mexico and met with PAN members—among them Alessandra Rojo—and governors María Eugenia Campos (who has an open investigation file for allowing the CIA into Chihuahua), María Teresa Jiménez, Libia Denisse, and Mauricio Kuri.

Alejandro Chafuen has a long track record in spaces that bring conservatives together, and particularly in the Atlas Network. Chafuen is a senior executive member of the Acton Institute and was president of the Atlas Network between 1991 and 2018. In his online profile he identifies himself as a disciple of Ludwig von Mises, an ideologue of orthodox economic theories taken up both by the Atlas Network and by Students for Liberty, as well as by conservative institutions such as Caminos de Libertad and Kybernus, the latter two linked to Ricardo Salinas Pliego. Since 1980, Chafuen has been a member of the Mont Pèlerin Society.

Kybernus is an organization belonging to Ricardo Salinas Pliego's network; it has an ideological-training profile for young people and has formed 35 local groups with 4,000 participants across the country. In Mexico City its promoters are Luis Rodolfo Nájera Ramírez and Karen Andrade Hernández. Kybernus has 41 promoters spread throughout the country.

This association was created back in 2011 and, according to its own website, states that "Mexico faces a crisis of leadership that has generated distrust, apathy, and stagnation." Kybernus is headed by Enrique Paniagua.

In addition, Ricardo Salinas is part of The Aspen Institute, where his daughter Ninfa Salinas is also a member, as are Leonardo Lomelí and former Foreign Affairs Secretary Juan Ramón de la Fuente. Among the economists linked to The Aspen are Luis de la Calle and Jaime Zabludovsky, both connected to México Evalúa, an organization that receives funding from the coup-mongering National Endowment for Democracy (NED), from the Atlas Network, from the Open Society Foundation, among others.

México Evalúa and IMCO have been two organizations opposed to the government of the Fourth Transformation since López Obrador began his administration, and they continue now under Claudia Sheinbaum. Another organization is Mexicans Against Corruption and Impunity (MCCI), of Claudio X. González and María Amparo Casar.

As Contralínea has extensively documented, MCCI received funding from the United States government to carry out supposedly journalistic activities that are in reality part of the media offensive—or, more precisely, of the manufacturing of consent against the Mexican government.

But it is not only these organizations: as this magazine documented as recently as 2025, despite the closure of USAID, funding continued to 35 NGOs, companies, and the ITAM.

CPAC is one of the international organizations that brings together the conservative right and that boosted Trump's candidacy. Mexican PAN politicians such as Raúl Torres Guerrero and former governor Francisco García Cabeza de Vaca have attended this organization. So too have Tania Larios of the PRI, and even Eduardo Verástegui, someone close to Ricardo Salinas.

Since 2024, the media war against the government of President Claudia Sheinbaum has been documented. Now the narrative of narco-government, narco-candidate, and narco-party returns, going from the "Marco Rubio List" to the Department of Justice's designation of the governor of Sinaloa, Rubén Rocha. Dr. Jorge Retana Yarto characterized this strategy as an offensive phase of limited chaos.

As part of the United States government's strategy, the media war is now considered part of the hybrid war to which the country is being subjected. As has been documented, USAID and the NED have paid various—supposedly independent—media outlets in Latin America for more than 200 articles. Among the outlets in Mexico, notable ones are the Center for Journalism and Public Ethics, Connectas, the International Center for Journalists, Border Hub, and Quinto Elemento Lab.

For example, Connectas conducted five investigations under NED sponsorship in 2019, and in 2020 there were 15 more. This outlet has ties to the International Center for Journalists, Innovation for Change, the International Republican Institute (IRI), the Konrad Adenauer Stiftung, Luminate, SembraMedia, and the Border Center (which also collaborates with USAID and the ICFJ).

The Konrad Adenauer foundation is a special case, since it is an ally of the Government and Political Analysis Organization, A.C. (GAPAC), directed by Dr. Liliana Obregón. Among GAPAC's allies are outlets such as El Toque of Cuba, Expediente Abierto, the Andrés Bello Foundation, Programa Cuba, and the Free America Forum—which brings us back to the PAN's ties to other kinds of organizations.

The Free America Forum has organized international meetings where PAN members such as Xóchitl Gálvez, Margarita Zavala, Santiago Taboada, Marko Cortés, and Mariana Gómez share the stage with the international right. For example, with Félix Madariaga of Nicaragua, director of RELIAL; Andrés Pastrana of Colombia; Jorge Quiroga, former president of Bolivia; Olenka Kondratiuk of Ukraine; and Rosa María Payá, an opponent of the Cuban government. It is a space of sociability that allows the PAN to gain international projection and to weave ties with other members of right-wing groups and parties.

Notable allies of the Free America Forum are the Konrad Adenauer foundation, which is also a partner of the outlet Diálogo Político; the IRI, linked to the NED; the CASLA Institute, Transparencia Electoral, the Foundation for Liberty; GAPAC, Atypical TeVe, México Unido, and Rescatemos México; the Miguel Hidalgo borough governed by Mauricio Tabe; and the National Civic Front, among others.

It is important to recall that the IRI funded courses, beginning in 2004, to increase political preparation and the participation of women in Mexican politics—also documented by Contralínea.

The National Civic Front, as is well known, counts among its ranks Guadalupe Acosta Naranjo—who added praise for Cayetana Álvarez's visit to Mexico in June 2026—Emilio Álvarez, and Emilio Sánchez Licona, among others.

The International Foundation for Liberty also includes Ricardo Salinas's Caminos de Libertad, RELIAL—which is headquartered in Mexico and connects with the Instituto Libertad—the Pro Libertad Institute, the Eleutera Foundation of Honduras—accused of encouraging destabilization against the government of Manuel Zelaya—the Democracy and Prosperity Foundation, and Libertank. Many of these organizations are linked to the Atlas Network Latin America network, directed by Roberto Salinas de León.

Little known is the organization Students for Liberty in Mexico. However, on its social media it tends to share Kybernus events such as the 2022 Encounter for Liberties, held in the Chamber of Deputies. Other organizations with which they interact include the Foundation for the Advancement of Liberty, part of the Atlas Network. Lastly, and linked to Atlas, is the Libertarian Party of Mexico, which contributes to the construction of the narco-government narrative.

The power network that the PAN embodies shows signs of a process of conservative rightward radicalization that links it with coup-mongering and fascist organizations, with foreign powers such as the United States, with members of its government such as Ambassador Ronald Johnson, with USAID, the NED, and the NGOs that have played a part in striking the Mexican government; and with Latin American former presidents who have indebted their countries, undermined democracy, and even called for invasions, as in the case of María Corina Machado.
