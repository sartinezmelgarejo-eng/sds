This article by La Jornada Correspondents originally appeared in the June 12, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left-wing daily newspaper.

Members of the Coordinadora Nacional de Trabajadores de la Educación (CNTE) continued their protests yesterday in eight states, in the context of the mobilization for the inaugural game of the 2026 FIFA World Cup. Their demands are the abrogation of the 2007 ISSSTE Law, a solidarity-based pension system, the elimination of the Afore system, and a 100 percent salary raise. Early on, teachers from Section 7 of the Sindicato Nacional de Trabajadores de la Educación (SNTE-CNTE) seized the Ángel Albino Corzo International Airport in Tuxtla Gutiérrez for more than five hours. With this action, they said, "we have shown that the compañeros are willing to take actions and activities if there is no response to our demands," said a member of the political leadership.

Sources from Section 7 commented that they also burned tires at the entrance of the air terminal, and although "a brewing scuffle and shoving" broke out between teachers and users, it did not escalate. They also commented that the occupation of the Petróleos Mexicanos storage and distribution plant and of the central park of the state capital continued.

In Sonora, groups of teachers stationed themselves at the toll booths of federal highway Mexico 15, between Hermosillo and Guaymas, to allow free passage to motorists and truckers. While they raised the toll arms, they distributed information about the movement's demands and requested solidarity support from travelers.

In Sinaloa, CNTE teachers demonstrated in Los Mochis, municipality of Ahome, where they demanded concrete responses from the federal government. One of the speakers criticized the claims that they have a better salary compared to past sexenios: "they want to deceive society by making them believe that we are millionaires, that we are rich," he asserted. The dissidents rejected the recent statements by sections 27 and 53 of the SNTE, which pointed out that the protests represent a minority of the teaching corps. "Of course we are going to be a minority if those who should defend us do not do so."

In Chihuahua, teachers from Section 8 of the SNTE, grouped in the Red de Defensa Magisterial, liberated four toll booths in the municipalities of Jiménez, Saucillo, Ahumada and the state capital; another group of about 50 teachers protested in the municipal seat of Madera. In Baja California, in the morning, teachers blocked the international Mesa de Otay border crossing, east of Tijuana, to impede vehicle passage from Mexico to the United States. The protest affected the transit of tractor trailers seeking to join the international export crossing toward the neighboring country, generating delays in circulation on one of the main commercial routes of the border.

In Nayarit, some 300 members of the CNTE marched in Tepic and gathered in the main plaza, where they also demanded the cessation of deductions applied to workers who have participated in the mobilizations. In Guerrero, teachers from the Coordinadora Estatal de Trabajadores de la Educación (Ceteg) again closed bank branches and department stores in Chilpancingo; in Acapulco they again blocked the Miguel Alemán coastal avenue, at the level of the Zócalo, from 10 a.m. to 12 p.m.; members of the Sindicato Único del Colegio de Bachilleres joined the protest.

In Morelos, some 30,000 members of Section 19 of the SNTE-CNTE and of the Movimiento Magisterial de Bases marched in Cuernavaca, divided into two contingents; when both groups converged at the plaza de armas, in front of the government palace, three brewing scuffles were registered between the two antagonistic expressions. The largest group — almost 20,000 teachers — was led by the secretary general of Section 19 of the SNTE, Joel Sánchez Vélez.
