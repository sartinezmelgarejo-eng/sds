This article by Arturo Sánchez and Alma E. Muñoz originally appeared in the June 16, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left wing daily newspaper.

Mexico City. Nearly four months after the operation in which federal forces killed Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, alias El Mencho, the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG) is experiencing a process of internal fragmentation into regional factions, a reconfiguration that so far has not resulted in widespread loss of control over violence due to the territorial structure of its leadership, the Secretary of Security and Citizen Protection, Omar García Harfuch, asserted this Tuesday.

At the morning press conference of President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo at the National Palace, the federal official explained that the criminal organization currently operates with well-defined territorial divisions, similar to the dynamic previously observed in other organized crime groups. "Yes, there are factions of this cartel as happened in the past with the Sinaloa cartel. So, as we mentioned ever since the Secretariat of National Defense carried out the operation, they have very well-identified regional leaders, with let's say very marked territories. That also allowed, in one way or another, for there to be no loss of control or excessive violence," the secretary detailed.

García Harfuch pointed out that the institutions of the security cabinet — which include the Secretariats of National Defense and the Navy — are continuing to track the regional commanders of the criminal group, several of whom have outstanding judicial warrants. "Later, arrests continued to be made by the Security Cabinet, by Defense, by the Navy, and there continue to be regional leaders who have arrest warrants, some with arrest warrants and also for purposes of extradition," he noted.

The head of public security in the country also stated that, after the loss of the CJNG's main leadership, the alliance that this organization maintained with a specific sector of the Sinaloa cartel was diluted. When questioned about this link, García Harfuch clarified that "it was mainly the leader himself, the principal leader, who was the direct link with a faction of the Sinaloa cartel, specifically with 'Los Chapos.' We do not have that identified at this time."

Likewise, the official validated the reports that point to Juan Carlos Valencia González, identified as 'El R3' and stepson of Oseguera Cervantes, as one of the central figures in the current dispute over control of the criminal structure, describing him as "one of the strongest regional leaders that the criminal group has."

Regarding the internal situation that the Sinaloa cartel is going through, the secretary described it as one of the most consolidated organizations in the territory, although he stressed that government operations have diminished its operational capacity in its principal zone of influence. "Of course, it is one of the strongest cartels in the country. However, only in Sinaloa, since the start of President Claudia Sheinbaum's administration, more than 6,000 firearms have been seized, more than 2,500 people detained for high-impact crimes. This represents a decrease or a direct impact on the different factions of the Sinaloa cartel," he specified.

He added that the dynamics of captures in the region reflect this weakening and the extension of operations to various criminal cells that make up the organization. "It has been changing. At the beginning of the administration, or last year, the great majority of detainees we had, for example, in Culiacán and its surroundings, were from a specific faction of the Chapitos, and now they are no longer; now they have been Chapitos, but also from other factions; that is, one group has been shrinking more," he expanded.

Finally, when addressing the general panorama on the proliferation of smaller-scale criminal gangs dedicated to illicit activities such as extortion and 'floor charges,' García Harfuch warned that there are local cells that operate autonomously but use the name of the large organizations for purposes of intimidation. "Of course, there are small local groups, sometimes unfortunately very violent, that hide behind, so to speak, the name of a larger criminal organization, although sometimes they have absolutely nothing to do with it. Not that in all cases they have nothing to do with it; there are cases where they do, but let's say they are not part of a cartel as such," he stated.

The Secretary of Security avoided offering a preliminary figure on the total number of criminal gangs and cartels operating under this scheme in the national territory in order not to fall into speculation, and offered that he will present a detailed breakdown by state once an exhaustive analysis of the regional situation is completed.
