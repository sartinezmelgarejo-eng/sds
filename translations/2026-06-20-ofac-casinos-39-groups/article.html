The US Office of Foreign Assets Control, in coordination with Mexico's Financial Intelligence Unit, are keeping under surveillance the 39 business groups that control casinos and betting establishments in Mexico, to rule out money laundering linked to drug trafficking. So far, OFAC and the UIF have sanctioned the casinos Centenario; Diamante —presumably linked to the Northeast Cartel—; Emine; Mirage; Midas; Palermo; Skampa —these last ones presumably tied to the Sinaloa Cartel. Mexican oversight includes video-recorded inspections of the betting halls by personnel from the Directorate of Games and Drawings of the Interior Secretariat (Segob), as well as the regularization of permits. For various irregularities —in particular, for having a greater number of slot machines—, in 2025 fines of 6 billion pesos were applied, while casino operators paid taxes (VAT and Income Tax) of 74 billion pesos.

The 39 groups authorized to operate betting halls, casinos and online betting in Mexico are under the lens of the US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), which a couple of years ago launched an exhaustive investigation to determine whether or not these businesses are involved in operations with funds of illicit origin, especially those coming from drug trafficking. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), an agency of the US Department of the Treasury, is also collaborating in these inquiries.

Among the businesses watched by US authorities are Grupo Caliente, owned by PRI politician Jorge Hank Rhon and with permit number 03923; and three corporations tied to media outlets: Apuestas Internacionales, SA de CV, with permit DGAJS/SCEVF/P 05/2005, a subsidiary of Grupo Televisa, SAB; Ganador Azteca, SAPI de CV, with permit DGJS/DGAAD/DCRCA/P 01/2018, of TV Azteca, which has already been sanctioned in Mexico; Superjuegos Robin, SA de CV, with permit DGJS/DCRCA/P 01/2021, whose legal representative —Roberto Elías Hernández— also served as legal director of Multimedios Televisión; and Eventos Festivos de México, SA de CV, whose owner would be Olegario Vázquez Aldir, owner of Grupo Imagen.

The inquiries are also coordinated with Mexican authorities —in particular with the Financial Intelligence Unit and the Tax Administration Service (SAT)— to exchange information, issue sanctions at the same time, and block bank accounts of both natural persons and legal entities associated with casinos that engage in the crime of capital laundering.

Both in Mexico and the United States, the activity related to the operation of casinos and physical and online betting halls is considered vulnerable to organized-crime money laundering and to white-collar crime. In our country, this is established in Article 17 of the Federal Law for the Prevention and Identification of Operations with Funds of Illicit Origin, and also includes games and drawings.

Ongoing Sanctions

So far, OFAC and the UIF have sanctioned the casinos: Centenario; Diamante —presumably linked to the Northeast Cartel—; Emine; Mirage; Midas; Palermo; Skampa —these last ones presumably tied to the Sinaloa Cartel. The online bookmakers Ganador Azteca, SAPI de CV, and Operadora Ganador Tv Azteca, SAPI de CV, owned by Grupo Salinas, were also suspended.

In the Mexican case, oversight of this economic sector includes video-recorded inspections of the betting halls by personnel from the Directorate of Games and Drawings —of the Secretariat of the Interior—, as well as the regularization of permits.

The results of these inspections have led to the detection of various irregularities —in particular, that casinos and betting halls have a greater number of slot machines than they report to the authority—. Derived from the detection of these anomalies, in 2025 fines of 6 billion pesos were applied. In addition, the more exhaustive review by the Tax Administration Service meant that the 39 casino-operator groups paid taxes (VAT and Income Tax) of 74 billion pesos last year.

The review carried out by Mexican authorities has also made it possible to identify the modus operandi of capital laundering, and the places where these businesses operate in closer proximity to organized crime, which increases their vulnerability.

According to the inquiries, the casinos that were laundering capital were located in Jalisco, Nuevo León, Sonora, Sinaloa, Chiapas, State of Mexico, Mexico City and Baja California, and had three types of patterns: 1) use of large amounts of cash; 2) networks that moved money coming from abroad within the country; and 3) digital operations such as transfers and payment platforms to hide the origin of the money.

The UIF, the SAT, the Federal Tax Prosecutor's Office and the Interior Secretariat documented unusual operations of up to 50 million pesos, as well as international transfers directed to financial-technology companies in the United States and various countries in Europe.

In particular, on November 12, 2025, the federal tax prosecutor Grisel Galeano García reported that the money-laundering scheme in casinos operated in six steps:

  1. A person is recruited through deception or identity theft (especially young students, retired workers or housewives), whose bank data are stolen;
  2. Through electronic means, they are sent prepaid cards or coded prizes with money of unknown origin for use in the casinos;
  3. With these resources, the people, sometimes unknowingly, place bets in the casinos physically or online;
  4. The casino system shows that this person won millions, however, they do not receive the winnings or do not learn that there were any, since the money is sent immediately to accounts abroad, to later be sent to tax havens where it is laundered or inserted into other bets to return it to the country;
  5. In some cases the people do receive the money from the supposed winnings in their accounts, but are asked to forward it or use it to make other operations;
  6. This process is repeated hundreds or thousands of times and with that the money is laundered.
