This article by Reyes Martínez Torrijos originally appeared in the June 24, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left wing daily newspaper.

At its traditional Adicción Tribal auction, the firm Millon & Associés is putting around 54 lots of Mexican cultural heritage items up for sale in Paris today. The French company expects to raise about 168,000 euros (more than 3,375,000 pesos) for these pieces. The objects linked to our country come from the Maya, Olmec, Teotihuacan, Mexica, Chontal, and Totonac cultures, from the civilizations of Tlatilco (State of Mexico), Mezcala (Jalisco), Chupícuaro (Michoacán), as well as from the states of Colima and Nayarit.

Adicción Tribal brings together 277 lots, among which objects from Africa, Europe, and America stand out, a continent from which come creations from Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras, and Peru.

With the highest estimated price in the auction (40,000 euros), this sale offers the Teotihuacan Cult mask of a dignitary with an introspective gaze, made of pale green limestone and dated between the years 450 and 650. According to the auction house's assessment, in it one observes that “the representation of the human face achieves a formal perfection based on balance and proportion.”

Among the most highly valued items related to Mexico, at 12,000 euros each, are the following: the Olmec Mask of a dignitary with a vigorous expression (900-550 BCE); Chicomecóatl, Aztec goddess of maize and abundance (1200-1521), and the Maya Domestic axe with a stylized iguana head (600-900).

From Veracruz, the so-called Important statue is offered, a representation of a lord in the fullness of his life wearing the belt of a ball player. Dated between the years 200 and 700, Millon expects to receive at least 7,000 euros for this piece.

A Maya terracotta vase, decorated with a band of glyphs, representing the hero twins of the Popol Vuh, dated between the years 550-950, has a similar valuation. Also for about 7,000 euros, a Maya vase showing a priest holding a deer is for sale; the piece includes the image of two large vessels associated with offerings or the ceremonial consumption of cacao.

The Adicción Tribal catalog records that the pieces come from several collections, among which that of the Frenchman André Martínez stands out, the son of Spanish immigrants and a man who developed a “distinguished international career in investment banking.” In 1970, according to the description, “he discovered the art of pre-Columbian America and this encounter struck him as a revelation. Not the whim of a wealthy collector, but the choice of a perceptive eye: that of a man who recognizes, in these terracotta pieces made more than two millennia ago, something that is his own.

“The terracotta pieces from western Mexico –Colima, Nayarit, Veracruz– attracted him by their carnal presence, their way of standing upright in the silence of the display cases, as they once did in rituals and offerings.”
