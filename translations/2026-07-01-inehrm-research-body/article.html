This article by Arturo Sánchez Jiménez and Alma E. Muñoz originally appeared in the July 1, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left wing daily newspaper.

Mexico City. With a call to strengthen critical thinking in the face of the advance of the far right, the federal government transformed the National Institute for Historical Studies of Mexico's Revolutions (INEHRM) into a decentralized public research and higher-education body, through a decree signed this Wednesday by President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo.

At her morning press conference at the National Palace, the president formalized the change by signing the decree with which the institute will cease to depend on the Ministry of Culture and be incorporated into the National System of Public Research Centers of the Ministry of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation (Secihti), keeping Felipe Ávila as director.

Sheinbaum explained that the transformation will make it possible to expand the body's functions, which in addition to carrying out historical research will offer bachelor's degrees and postgraduate studies.

The President stressed that the creation of the new body will not entail any harm to other public research centers. "Let it not be said that this institute is at the expense of the others... this year we are opening 200 positions for those institutes," she maintained.

She highlighted that it was decided to make the change on this July 1 "because eight years ago was the triumph of the Fourth Transformation of Mexico's Public Life, and the way to recognize it, the triumph of the people, well, is this Institute that is dedicated to history, to the research of the revolutions in our country."

The Secretary of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation, Rosaura Ruiz Gutiérrez, pointed out that one of the lines of work entrusted by the president will be the analysis of the advance of the far right. "One topic among many that the president has indicated to us is to pay attention to and research the advance of the far right in different parts of the world. In the face of hate speech that seeks to exacerbate classism, racism, and social division, the history and memory of our people must be a moral barrier," she affirmed.

For his part, Felipe Ávila, who holds a doctorate in History from El Colegio de México, reported that the institute will initially offer bachelor's degrees in history, social sciences, and humanities, as well as in public administration and good governance, in addition to master's degrees and specializations.

He also announced the formation of a collegiate academic body made up of Enrique Semo, Lorenzo Meyer, Armando Bartra, Romana Falcón, Ariel Rodríguez Kuri, and Margarita Vázquez Montaño, among other specialists. "It is a collegiate body made up of figures of recognized trajectory and progressive thought within Mexican academia," he pointed out.

He added that the group will also incorporate new generations of researchers and Latin American intellectuals to broaden academic exchange.

In that context, he maintained that the purpose is to strengthen regional discussion in the face of "the rise of right-wing and far-right movements in Latin America," and affirmed that they seek to "close ranks to stop these threats to civilization posed by the rise of the right and the far right in Latin America, but also in the world."

Ávila reported that, following the signing of the presidential decree, the admissions call will be published this month in order to begin academic activities in September, first at a site in the Historic Center of Mexico City and later at the former convent of Jesús María.
