This article by Nancy Flores originally appeared in the August 1, 2026 edition of Contralínea, a Mexican investigative journalism magazine.

The environmental impact assessment—carried out by the López Obrador government—reveals that Calica, a subsidiary of the U.S. company Vulcan Materials, is responsible for irreversible damage to biodiversity in the municipalities of Solidaridad and Cozumel, Quintana Roo, both inside and outside the area licensed for limestone extraction (1,251.43 hectares), meaning the environmental impact would exceed 2,000 hectares. This caused harm to 883 species of flora and fauna—194 of them in danger of extinction—as well as to the area's underground hydrological system—destroying three cenotes and partially or totally contaminating the water in another 14—and contamination of the soils with 14 cancer-causing heavy metals. The assessment estimated that restoring the zone, located in a biological corridor that extends as far as Campeche, would exceed 7 billion dollars, an amount the company would have to cover. This week, after ICSID indicated that its ruling did not recognize most of Vulcan Materials' demands following the shutdown of operations ordered during the previous administration, President Sheinbaum stressed that the transnational must compensate Mexico for the environmental damage caused.

Harm to 883 species of flora and fauna, of which 128 are at risk and 66 are in the CITES category (such as the loggerhead turtle, the keel-billed toucan and the red mangrove); loss of medium-maturity forest, of germplasm typical of tropical forests, and of habitat for at-risk species (such as the howler monkey, spider monkey, great curassow, ahan-ché, amapa and nakás palm); fragmentation of the biological corridor of flagship species (among them the jaguar, currently in danger of extinction); and a reduction in carbon-capture zones, are some of the worst environmental impacts that the U.S. firm Vulcan Materials caused across more than 2,000 hectares located in the municipalities of Solidaridad and Cozumel, Quintana Roo.

The 1,251.43-hectare area is divided into four plots located in that state, considered one of the richest in biodiversity in the world. This property of the subsidiary Calica (Calizas Industriales del Carmen, SA de CV, now called Sac-Tun) shows 80 percent deforestation in the Corchalito plot; 82 percent deforested area in the La Rosita plot; modification of the coastal profile in the port zone (plot 3); and a 100 percent conserved area in the La Adelita plot, according to an inter-institutional investigation carried out during the government of President Andrés Manuel López Obrador.

The assessment estimates that the damage caused to biodiversity, to water bodies and to the region's soil—which has spread beyond Vulcan Materials' land, since it would affect more than 2,000 hectares—amounts to some 7 billion dollars that the transnational would have to pay the Mexican State.

This is because that assessment reports that Calica (or Sac-Tun) is also responsible for irreversible impacts on the underground hydrological system inside and outside the area (of 1,251.43 hectares) that was licensed to it in 1986, by the government of Miguel de la Madrid, for limestone extraction. These impacts include the destruction of at least three cenotes and, in another 14, the partial or total contamination of the water by dust, exposure of the water table to the open air, mixing of saline and fresh waters, limestone fractures in surrounding areas, high presence of nitrogen from nitrites and other compounds, as well as changes in underground flows due to pressure differences.

Scientific reports point out that in the area there is an interruption of the regional underground flow due to the large water storages inside Calica; which was verified by the drop in the water-table level of the aquifer in coastal cenotes. In the latter, a deterioration in water quality directly attributable to mining activity is reported.

Regarding the impacts outside the U.S. transnational's property, it is indicated that the modification of regional flows generated subsidence, cracks and depressions along Highway 307 in front of Calica, which in karst zones can produce sinkholes.

As for contamination, the assessment specifies that it was detected not only in the water, but also in the soils dynamited and destroyed for limestone extraction. Based on scientific tests, the report demonstrates the presence of 14 heavy metals, associated with terminal illnesses such as cancer and kidney deficiencies. These are: barium, chromium, cobalt, aluminum, copper, iron, potassium, magnesium, manganese, lead, sodium, nickel, titanium and vanadium.

It also notes that "the alteration and destruction of caverns and karst landscape inside the plot was verified"; and that "the water bodies artificially created inside the plot constitute a retention of groundwater far above what is authorized in the concessions granted by Conagua. [...] With the above, it is evident that the company SAC-TUN (formerly CALICA) has engaged in an illegal appropriation of water resources, since there are undeclared volumes of water and no record of payments of guarantee fees, which amount to 136.87 million pesos as of today [2022]."

International Tribunal Ruled in Mexico's Favor

The environmental disaster the transnational left in Quintana Roo became the substance of the international arbitration that Vulcan Materials itself resorted to, on May 18, 2020. The international tribunal—the World Bank's International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes—before which it sued the Mexican State for alleged harm to its business activity—a claim it expanded in May 2022, after the López Obrador government, through Profepa, shut down its activities—assessed that the company has no arguments to continue extracting limestone in the devastated area.

The conclusion of the international arbitration derives from the fact that Mexico, through its federal institutions, managed to demonstrate that the negative impacts on the environment and human health stem from Calica's extractive activities in the zone, which included illegal limestone extraction below the water table, an activity that was never authorized to it.

During the arguments process, the previous federal administration even carried out a helicopter tour of the devastated zone so that the representatives of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes could directly observe the more than 2,000 hectares devastated by Calica. Sources consulted by Contralínea reveal that, previously, the ICSID arbitrators had already seen images and videos provided by Mexico, but they were shocked to directly observe the "craters" formed by the explosions that Vulcan's subsidiary recurrently carried out to extract more stone material than authorized.

For this reason, in its final ruling—issued this past July 27—ICSID is emphatic against the extractivist company: according to the Secretariat of Economy, all the claims of Vulcan Legacy LLC—the transnational's subsidiary that led this international arbitration, under NAFTA—were dismissed, except for one measure related to the closure of a Calica plot, which occurred in January 2018, it explained. In a press release, the federal agency added that "this measure represents less than 1 percent of the original amount claimed." This is because Mexico will have to pay only 15 million dollars, out of the 1.7 billion dollars that the transnational demanded.

Future Legal Actions Against Vulcan

After this significant international ruling, the federal government will be able to undertake legal actions against Vulcan Materials for the serious and irreversible damage caused to the environment, in line with what was announced by President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo. And at her morning press conference this past July 28, the president affirmed that the transnational must compensate Mexico for the environmental harm. Although she anticipated that details of the case cannot yet be given, until ICSID makes its final ruling official.

Our country's economic claim against the company could reach 7 billion dollars, an amount that the inter-institutional investigation carried out during the López Obrador government estimated as the cost of the damage. That assessment—led by Semarnat, together with institutions such as Conanp, the IMTA and the INECC—evaluated that the impacts on biodiversity require that multimillion-dollar fund to partially restore the more than 2,000 affected hectares, since there are damages that are irreversible.

For example, it was concluded that in the case of the devastated vegetation, more than 300 years would be required for its recovery; regarding the loss of organic soils, the multidisciplinary investigation indicates that their restoration requires "thousands of years"; and in the case of the water tables, there is not even an estimate in years.

Calica's Great Devastation

The environmental disaster in Quintana Roo attributed to Vulcan Materials—for which it lost the international arbitration and must now compensate Mexico—is, above all, a violation of the human right to a healthy environment, states the federal analysis prepared in 2022 and consulted by Contralínea. The devastation derives not only from the limestone extraction that was authorized to it during the neoliberal period, but also from activities that Semarnat itself determined, four years ago, to be illegal. In particular, because Calica extracted limestone below the water table, which was never authorized to it.

The report explains that to extract and use limestone below the water table, first comes the clearing and stripping of the forest, which causes deforestation. Then boreholes are drilled, which modifies or interrupts the regional underground flow, deteriorates the quality of the water and contaminates it, in addition to generating cracks and depressions.

It also involves loading with explosives, blasting and extraction of the stone material, activities associated with those same impacts on the water tables and cenotes, and with the destruction and contamination of soils. Added to this is that this type of "use" of limestone requires transport, crushing, screening, material selection and washing, which contributes to contaminating water and soils. Finally comes storage, shipment and distribution.

Vulcan's Economic Debt to Mexico

It is for all this that in the previous six-year term, Semarnat and other federal institutions—including Conagua—estimated that Vulcan Materials should compensate Mexico, for this damage, the 7 billion dollars. This, to restore as far as possible the affected biological corridor.

In the case of soil restoration alone, it was determined that the economic cost per hectare amounted to 60,658,181.82 pesos (at 2022 value), not including the artificial water bodies. "It has been calculated that the social cost for restoring the zone impacted by the mining activity of Calica, today Sac-Tun, amounts to 34,650,986,364.68 pesos," states the Environmental Impact Assessment derived from the industrial limestone extraction project run by the company Calica (today Sac-Tun) in the municipalities of Solidaridad and Cozumel, Quintana Roo.

This is because "from 1985 to 2022, 926.71 hectares of primary vegetation of medium semi-evergreen (73.6 percent) and semi-deciduous (25 percent) forest have been transformed in the El Corchalito and La Rosita plots of the Calica industrial complex, today Sac-Tun. The impact on the CALICA plots, directly attributed to soil removal, has been of such magnitude that the regeneration of their vegetation cover is technically impossible."

The assessment adds that "restoration work can be promoted for a partial recovery of soil conditions that could eventually allow some level of recovery of some environmental functions and services in the zone, although it is reiterated that the environmental damage produced is practically irreversible."

Another estimate it offers refers to the loss of the forest. In this regard, it notes that "the devastation of the vegetation in the study area has also meant a decrease in the potential for carbon sequestration and storage, with the economic and social impacts that this implies. There is a potential loss of annual income from the total tons of carbon that could not be avoided and that could have entered carbon markets, calculated at 1.6 million dollars."

It adds that "the vegetation cover existing in the study area prior to 1985, corresponding to semi-evergreen forests, had a potential for the exploitation of timber and non-timber resources that could have been extracted under sustainable forestry schemes. It is estimated that in the polygon of the Calica plots, an annual area of 116.51 hectares could have been used, with a potential annual production of 24,855,579.66 pesos. This activity would have generated local economic and social benefits and would have allowed the conservation of the different environmental services of the zone."

Protected Area

On September 23, 2024, and as one of his last actions for the protection of biodiversity, then-President Andrés Manuel López Obrador issued in the Official Gazette of the Federation the "Decree declaring the Felipe Carrillo Puerto natural protected area, with the category of flora and fauna protection area, the surface of 53,227-07-02.37 hectares, located in the municipalities of Solidaridad, Cozumel and Tulum, state of Quintana Roo."

The protected area has since then encompassed the land owned by Vulcan Materials, as part of an ambitious strategy that sought to shield the region, in case ICSID ruled against Mexico and in favor of the transnational.

Among other things, the decree notes that the Felipe Carrillo Puerto site is home to 1,063 native species: seven prokaryotes, 31 protoctists, 437 vascular plants, 170 invertebrates and 418 vertebrates, a figure equivalent to 15 percent of the species registered in Quintana Roo. "Within this richness, 85 endemic species are recorded, 112 species in a risk category in accordance with the 'Official Mexican Standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010, Environmental protection—Native species of Mexico of wild flora and fauna—Risk categories and specifications for their inclusion, exclusion or change—List of species at risk,' published in the DOF on December 30, 2010 [...]."

Likewise, it establishes that the protected area presents nine types of vegetation: high or medium semi-evergreen forest, high or medium semi-deciduous forest, low semi-deciduous forest, low spiny evergreen forest, savanna, low semi-evergreen forest (savanna forest), mangrove, palm grove and coastal scrub. "The conservation of these plant communities is relevant in protecting ecosystems that are characteristic of the Neotropical region. In addition, this vegetation cover forms a continuum of forests characteristic of the Yucatán Peninsula Biogeographic Province."

Another of the characteristics that the protected-area declaration sought to protect and conserve is that of the coastal wetlands: "the savanna and the mangrove, which provide environmental services by being areas for feeding, refuge and growth of invertebrate and fish larvae, act as natural flood-control systems and as barriers against hurricanes and saline intrusion, control erosion, improve water quality by functioning as a biological filter, maintain sedimentation processes and serve as a refuge for wild flora and fauna. Likewise, the savannas contribute approximately 30 percent of terrestrial net primary productivity and, together with the tropical forests, are one of the ecosystems with the greatest plant biodiversity."
