This editorial originally appeared in the August 5, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left wing daily newspaper.

The federal government's proposal to establish general guidelines for the Protection of the Rights of Audiences has provoked a virulent reaction among the large corporate media, right-wing politicians, and para-business organizations misidentified as belonging to "civil society." These actors have unleashed an intense disinformation campaign with which they seek to convince the public that the authorities intend to impose mechanisms of prior censorship, regulate content, and reserve for themselves the power to establish the truth.

None of these accusations has anything to do with what is being promoted by the Telecommunications Regulatory Commission (CRT). As its presiding commissioner, Norma Solano Rodríguez, has explained, codes of ethics and editorial criteria are and will be defined by the media outlets themselves, while the figure of the audience ombudsperson will function only as an intermediary between the licensees and the public, issuing recommendations in the event that the public presents complaints, which is why "freedom of expression is guaranteed and the main objective is to strengthen the rights of audiences with clear and transparent procedures."

President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo has expressed herself in the same vein, stressing that no sanction is contemplated for the content that the media broadcast, but rather for failing to issue a code of ethics, failing to appoint an audience ombudsperson, and failing to make defense mechanisms available to audiences.

What is at stake, then, is the creation of self-regulation tools that make it possible to contrast questionable claims, enrich points of view, and provide the entities dedicated to communication with instruments to recognize their own excesses. It is worth noting that audience ombudsman offices have existed since the 1960s in the United States, and are currently institutionalized in much of Europe, Australia, New Zealand, and India. In the Anglophone world, their presence stands out in outlets such as The Washington Post, the United States' NPR (National Public Radio), The Guardian, and the BBC. Whether they are public entities or, as is proposed in Mexico, departments within the structure of the media outlets themselves, audience ombudsman offices have among their functions to receive, process, and manage the public's complaints, claims, and suggestions regarding content; to serve as an independent mediator and arbiter between the editorial team of a news organization and its public; and to point out journalistic errors, explain the processes or decisions behind the news, and verify compliance with codes of ethics.

They are grounded in the principle that readers, viewers, or listeners are not passive entities, but citizens with the right to receive content that reflects the ideological, political, and cultural plurality of their societies. This includes the right to have the media clearly distinguish news information from opinion and from advertising content, something that the corporate press tends to deliberately omit because the confusion between propaganda and information is the core of its business model.

Behind the campaign against the protection of audiences lies the desire to maintain a situation of total impunity in order to keep using media power for the benefit of the interest groups surrounding the media: their owners and shareholders, political allies, and companies benefited by favorable coverage, among others. Those who refuse to empower their audiences are accustomed to slandering for extortive ends, presenting false versions of events to favor their friends, harm their enemies, and distort reality to maximize their profits or those of their clients.

In sum, the public needs to reject the fallacies about censorship, make the debate its own, and demand accountability from those who hold in their hands an excessive power, capable of destroying lives and livelihoods, creating or annihilating political careers, and boycotting government programs, social movements, or rival companies. Empowering those who seek to exercise their right to information does not limit freedom; on the contrary, it consolidates a genuine democracy by turning the media monologue into a conversation.
