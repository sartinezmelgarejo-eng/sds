This article by La Redacción and AP originally appeared in the August 8, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left-wing daily newspaper.

The United States government agreed to partially resume its phytosanitary surveillance work in five municipalities of Michoacán, which will allow avocado exports to that country to resume starting this Saturday.

The announcement comes a day after Mexico announced the deployment of 1,557 troops from the Army and the National Guard (GN) to Michoacán to reinforce security and take part, together with local authorities, in the inspection of packing centers and communication routes, and to provide accompaniment and surveillance at the sanitary authorities' inspection points.

Earlier, the director of the Association of Avocado Producers and Exporting Packers of Mexico (APEAM), Luis Javier de la Rocha, told the press that an agreement had been reached with US authorities to release more than a thousand tons of avocados for export.

As the edition closed, Mexico's Secretariat of Agriculture had not commented on the announcement made by the US ambassador, Ronald Johnson.

In the morning, President Claudia Sheinbaum said in advance that her government would hold a meeting later with the US representation, to seek an alternative that would allow the quality inspection of avocados to continue, and assured that the matter of the fruit's production and marketing would be addressed on an interinstitutional basis.

She offered that the inspection be carried out either through the inspectors assigned by the United States –to meet the export requirements– or with personnel from the Secretariat of Agriculture, to perform this same surveillance work. She commented that envoys from the Secretariats of Security, Agriculture, Foreign Affairs, and Finance would take part in the meeting.

Last Wednesday, the US government suspended the activities of its personnel and the Department of Agriculture's inspections in Michoacán, after receiving a security alert related to threats against its interests and officials in the state.

The governor of Michoacán, Alfredo Ramírez Bedolla, reported on social media that the shutdown had been to safeguard the integrity of US workers, following recent arrests linked to extortion.

The US ambassador stressed yesterday that, based on the commitments made by the Mexican government, the security measures "and our ongoing assessment of conditions on the ground," they will resume official work in Tancítaro, Tacámbaro, Uruapan, and the corridor between Morelia and Pátzcuaro.

Johnson noted that they will continue working "closely," as part of a continuous and gradual process, in order to create the necessary conditions to fully restore his government's operations.

In his statement released by the diplomatic mission, he also thanked Mexico for its cooperation and the actions it has undertaken to strengthen the security of both US personnel and the population of Michoacán.

The US government classifies the state as a level 4 zone, the highest travel-alert category, because "there is a risk of violence in the state from terrorist groups, cartels, gangs, and criminal organizations."

On three occasions, the United States has suspended avocado exports over matters related to threats or incidents against personnel connected to US inspections.

The first time occurred on February 11, 2022, after a telephone threat against a phytosanitary worker, and lasted a week; the second was on June 15, 2024, when two inspectors were detained at a roadblock in Aranza, Paracho.
