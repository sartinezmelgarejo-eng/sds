This article by Darren García originally appeared in the August 18, 2026 edition of Contralínea, an independent Mexican investigative journalism magazine.

More than 1 million basic-education teachers, in more than 200,000 schools across the country, will give their opinions this coming August 24 and 25, during the intensive phase of the School Technical Councils, on the presence of digital devices in classrooms, announced the Secretary of Public Education, Mario Delgado Carrillo, at Monday's morning press conference.

The teacher consultation is the first step in a broader discussion on how to regulate the use of digital platforms among children and adolescents, President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo explained for her part.

The president recalled that 16 states of the Republic already have some regulation on the use of cell phones in schools, and that the goal is to extend it to the entire country, but first teachers, who have direct contact with mothers and fathers, will be asked for their opinion.

Sheinbaum Pardo announced that, once that phase is completed, the government will move on to discuss how to prevent the excessive use of the platforms, the unsolicited contact of adults with minors, exposure to unsolicited content, including sexual abuse, and the phenomenon of infinite scrolling, which she called an addiction that "is causing so many psychological problems in girls, boys, and adolescents."

Mario Delgado also extended an invitation to the first of three regional forums on the subject, scheduled for this Wednesday, August 19, at 9:00 a.m., at the Autonomous University of Nuevo León, in Monterrey, with invited specialists.

According to Ravi Iyer, director of the Psychology of Technology Institute and of the Neely Center at the University of Southern California, who worked four and a half years at Meta as a leader of research teams on Facebook's impact on society, the platforms' algorithms are usually designed to maximize connection time instead of being oriented toward users' real aspirations.

According to data cited by the specialist, 70 percent of young people say they feel manipulated by these platforms; another survey found that 45 percent acknowledge spending too much time on social media; the same percentage said it affects their sleep, and 40 percent that it harms their productivity.

Iyer also cited internal Meta research, made public in a lawsuit in New Mexico: 13 percent of Instagram users between 13 and 15 years old received some unwanted sexual advance over a seven-day period, and 19.2 percent of that same age group reported having seen nudity or unsolicited sexual content in a single week.

In Australia, a survey of more than 3,000 children between 10 and 17 years old found that 47 percent had seen offensive or sexist comments against girls and women, the same percentage saw videos of fights, and 46 percent, dangerous challenges.

Iyer detailed that a New Mexico court recently issued a final ruling against Meta Platforms, the second this year to hold a social media company legally responsible for the harms derived from the design of its platforms, and ordered that, for five years, the company strengthen age verification, hide by default the public "like" count from minors, disable nighttime and school-hours notifications, limit the combined use time on Facebook and Instagram, keep teenagers' accounts private, and prevent adults with no prior relationship from contacting minors or being recommended their profiles.

The specialist noted that the European Commission determined about a month ago that the addictive design of Instagram and Facebook violates the bloc's Digital Services Act, while Indonesia restricted the accounts of minors under 16 on platforms with harmful features and Canada adopted similar measures.
