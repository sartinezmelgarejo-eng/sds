This article by Josafat Hernández originally appeared in the August 21, 2026 edition of Contralínea, one of Mexico’s leading independent investigative magazines.

Mexico is going through a paradoxical economic juncture. On the one hand, the country has managed to attract historic levels of foreign direct investment (FDI). In 2025, FDI reached 40.871 billion dollars —a record figure— and the fifth consecutive all-time high. However, this figure must be interpreted with care: of that amount, around 27.649 billion corresponded to reinvested earnings, 5.844 billion to inter-company accounts, and barely 7.377 billion to new investments.

The figure confirms the appeal Mexico retains for international capital, but it also compels us to ask a question: what is happening with the productive investment of the national private business sector? Among the factors that explain the dynamism of foreign investment are the trade war between the United States and China, the relocation of production chains, the so-called nearshoring, the economic integration of North America, and the advantages Mexico offers thanks to its proximity to the U.S. market and its insertion into global value chains. Sectors such as automotive, electronics, transport equipment, and various manufacturing branches continue to be particularly relevant.

For this reason, it is not appropriate to explain capital flows into Mexico solely by the interest rate differential. An important part of the investment is linked to productive activities and to long-term business strategies. Mexico retains significant advantages: an economy highly integrated with the United States, a broad manufacturing base, accumulated productive infrastructure, and a large internal market.

All of this is also happening in a very complex international context. The trade war driven by the United States against China (since Trump's first administration), the rise in tariffs, the uncertainty surrounding the future of free trade, geopolitical conflicts, and the war between the United States and Iran have increased the uncertainty of the world economy. Tension around the Strait of Hormuz has, moreover, introduced new risks for energy markets and international trade.

In this international scenario, Mexico has managed to maintain a relatively favorable position. Part of Mexico's capacity to hold its position is its ability to adapt to uncertainty on the basis of the economic policies implemented during the governments of the Fourth Transformation. The increase in real wages, the expansion of social programs, and the strengthening of the internal market have helped sustain aggregate demand. At the same time, public investment in infrastructure has sought to expand the productive and logistical capacities of the entire country.

The strategic projects promoted in recent years —among them the Maya Train, the Interoceanic Corridor of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, energy infrastructure, and the new public investments in infrastructure— are part of a strategy aimed at strengthening national productive capacities. To this is added Plan México, which seeks to increase national production, strengthen the internal market, reduce certain external dependencies, and promote new investments.

The Economic Development Poles for Well-being are a concrete expression of this strategy. Their purpose is to articulate infrastructure, investment, innovation, and territorial planning, by combining public and private efforts, and to offer tax incentives and administrative facilities to attract new productive activities. There are currently 14 poles in various states of the country. And this has managed to establish the conditions to generate synergies among the various economic agents.

The State, therefore, is creating conditions to stimulate productive investment. And here appears one of the great contradictions of the Mexican economy today: while the country manages to attract foreign investment and the State deploys policies to expand productive capacities, an important part of the national business sector seems not to respond with the same dynamism.

The problem is not that no Mexican entrepreneur invests. Evidently there are national companies that expand their capacities, develop technology, create jobs, and actively participate in the country's productive transformation. The problem is broader: national private investment does not yet seem to match the magnitude of the opportunities and incentives that the Mexican economy offers. At present, Mexico has a total investment, as a percentage of GDP, of 21.2 percent, while China has 41 percent. In Mexico, private investment represents close to 90 percent of the total, while public investment represents barely 10 percent of the total. Moreover, without innovation, there will be little economic growth. Mariana Mazzucato and Lara Merling already said it in a recent report: spending on research and development represents around 0.20 percent of GDP, and of that, the share of private investment is less than 0.04 percent of GDP.

This situation is particularly striking because the State is assuming an important part of the risk associated with technological development and innovation. The Mexican State builds infrastructure, expands connectivity, sustains demand through social policies, raises wages, develops strategic projects, and offers incentives to attract capital, in addition to financing the bulk of research and development. The private sector, especially the national one, should take advantage of these conditions to expand its productive capacities, develop new technologies, and raise the national content of value chains. It should also invest more in research and development to generate more innovation.

However, the opposite frequently happens. A part of the business sector concentrates its discourse on uncertainty, regulation, or institutional changes, while demanding greater tax incentives, subsidies, concessions, and preferential conditions to invest. It is legitimate for businesspeople to demand certainty and clear rules; what is questionable is that the demand for favorable conditions becomes a permanent strategy to transfer to the State part of the risks that belong to business activity itself.

Here a fundamental contradiction of Mexican capitalism appears. The entrepreneur, in economic theory, is the one who assumes risks, organizes productive factors, innovates, and seeks new investment opportunities. But when a part of the business sector obtains its profits fundamentally from rents, concessions, oligopolistic positions, or benefits derived from public policies, it stops behaving like a productive entrepreneur in the strict sense and comes closer to a purely rentier logic. And here the problem appears when private capital intends to benefit from the infrastructure, incentives, and stability generated by the State without simultaneously assuming an equivalent responsibility in terms of investment, innovation, and productive development. Hence entrepreneurs like Ricardo Salinas Pliego represent an economic parasitism that does not benefit the country.

The problem is not that the State participates in the economy. In the moral economy, the market does not replace the State. Moreover, international experience shows that successful processes of industrialization can hardly be explained without an active participation of the State. In addition, the economies that grow the most are those that have a more strengthened State and that make large productive investments, as is the case of China, whose public sector owns close to 40 percent of the GDP of the world's largest economy.

Mexico needs an economically more solid government, and a more demanding relationship between the State and private capital. The government must generate infrastructure, energy security, workforce training, quality productive forces, scientific research, financing, and adequate regulatory conditions; but the business sector must respond with investment, innovation, the creation of quality jobs, and greater integration of national suppliers.

The international juncture offers an extraordinary opportunity. The reorganization of global supply chains can turn Mexico into one of the great beneficiaries of the new productive configuration of North America. But that opportunity cannot be seized solely through foreign investment. If national companies do not increase their participation in the new production chains, Mexico runs the risk of becoming a manufacturing platform for foreign capital without sufficiently developing a national business core capable of appropriating a greater part of the value generated.

That is why it is necessary to change Mexican business culture. It is not about confronting the private sector with the State, but about building a different relationship between the two. The State cannot be merely a provider of infrastructure, subsidies, and incentives, while the business sector limits itself to waiting for ideal conditions to invest. Mexico needs entrepreneurs willing to assume risks, innovate, and bet on national productive development. It needs companies that invest in technology, research, training, and new productive capacities. It needs, in short, for national capital to stop looking at the State exclusively as a source of benefits and to begin seeing it as a strategic partner in a development project. Public investment is indispensable, but not sufficient. Foreign investment can contribute capital, technology, and access to markets, but it is not sufficient either. Mexico needs a more nationalist and humanist business class that rises to the level of the economic transformation the country is experiencing.

The challenge consists of turning the current juncture of productive relocation, public investment, and strengthening of the internal market into a true process of national development. This requires a State with the capacity for economic leadership, but also a national private sector willing to invest, innovate, and assume risks. Economic sovereignty cannot be built solely from the government. It also requires a national business sector willing to participate actively in it.
