This article by Bárbara Zamora, a litigating lawyer, is the second part of a column that originally appeared in the August 22, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico’s premier left-wing daily newspaper.

1. A priori, it must be taken into account that the neoliberal Salinas-era reform of constitutional Article 27, carried out in 1992, radically altered the model of social justice, ended land redistribution, brought ejidal and communal lands into the market like any ordinary commodity, and eliminated agrarian constitutionalism. It suppressed essential functions such as the redistributive one, which allowed the government to expropriate large estates and distribute land to peasants, and it eliminated the special protection that social property held as inalienable, imprescriptible, and unseizable. The historic, inalienable, and fundamental right achieved through the Revolution of 1910 was unjustifiably eliminated through a power grab.

This reform, and all the legal and material effects it has generated in the countryside and in the property regime of ejidal and communal lands, persist and are worsening today. As a result, the companies that carry out megaprojects of all kinds can seize whatever lands they want without any legal problem, having a legal and constitutional framework that favors them. This reform thus became the legal root of dispossession.

At the same time, new institutions were created, such as the agrarian courts, the agrarian attorney’s office, and a new Agrarian Law. The agrarian courts replaced the President of the Republic as the “highest agrarian authority,” with the functions of issuing rulings and resolving the case files that were pending at that time, as well as resolving new agrarian disputes, all of which, before the constitutional reform, was handled by the Secretariat of Agrarian Reform through an administrative procedure.

Under the LGPIA, powers are now granted to the head of the Federal Executive to sign presidential decrees and thereby recognize and title the communal property of Indigenous peoples and communities, which the law calls “traditional communal property,” and it states that “the head of the Federal Executive may recognize and title these lands by means of a decree issued in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution, this law, the Agrarian Law, and the other applicable laws.”

Article 109 forgets that, in the case of the lands of Indigenous communities, there currently exist two procedures for titling their property: the restitution of lands that have been taken from them, and the Recognition and Titling of Communal Property. These actions are handled in the agrarian courts, as established by constitutional Article 27. This provision is therefore inconsistent with what constitutional Articles 89 and 27 and the Agrarian Law establish on the matter. To begin with, it must be recalled that the powers of the President of the Republic are described in Article 89 of the Constitution, and in none of the 17 sections that comprise it is the President granted the power to issue decrees on the Recognition and Titling of Communal Property, or “traditional-property lands,” as they are now called.

It is also stated that this will be “in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution, the Agrarian Law, and other laws.” Which Constitution is being referred to? Because, as we have already noted, Article 89 does not grant these powers to the Federal Executive, and in Article 27 this authority is reserved for the agrarian courts.

Besides being inconsistent, it is also confusing, because it is not clarified whether, now that the head of the Federal Executive is going to issue decrees on these case files, this replaces the agrarian courts in that function and, consequently, an office like the former Secretariat of Agrarian Reform will be created to process such files.

In conclusion, this is a rule that makes no sense at all and that in reality seeks to relaunch, as if they were novel, presidential resolutions as a means to title the lands of Indigenous communities and peoples without any legal basis. If the decrees in question were in fact issued, it would be an eminently political act to showcase the sitting president, as in the remote days of the PRI.

2. Land and territory: Article 108 provides that Indigenous peoples and communities “have the right to territorial integrity,” which comprises “lands, the environment, and natural resources.” In this regard, it must be clarified that there is a fundamental difference between the right to territory and the right of ownership of land. And in this sense, we return to what constitutional Article 27 establishes, where three types of property are expressly recognized: ejidal, communal, and small property. The notion of Indigenous territory is not provided for in the Constitution. Article 27 sharply separates ownership of the land from ownership of what is above or below it. There is a dissociation between land and natural resources. Natural resources (water, minerals, hydrocarbons, marine resources, and so on) are the property of the nation, as stated in the first, fourth, fifth, and sixth paragraphs.

The 1992 neoliberal reform of Article 27 and the Agrarian Law established a logic of fragmentation and commodification of ejidal- and communal-regime lands, as well as of all the natural resources and minerals found on those lands, which are granted in concession to commercial companies for long periods.

In light of what is set out in Article 27, it can be concluded that the agrarian property of Indigenous communities does not automatically equate to ownership of the territory, because of the division that exists in the Constitution between ownership of the surface of the land and everything the territory comprises, of which Indigenous communities and peoples hold only the right to use and enjoyment.

In the San Andrés dialogues between the Zapatista Army of National Liberation and the federal government, the reform of constitutional Article 27 was included as one of the agenda items approved within the framework of the Law for a Just and Dignified Peace in Chiapas of March 1995, and this reform, along with other items on that agenda, remains pending to this day.

A radical and urgent reform of constitutional Article 27 would have to be carried out to return to the spirit and letter of the 1917 constituents, taking up the social justice originally embodied in the Constitution of 1917, since the neoliberal reform carried out in 1992 turned Article 27 into an impassable legal wall against which the collective rights of Indigenous peoples and communities collide. Otherwise, the rights established by this law will be merely symbolic.
