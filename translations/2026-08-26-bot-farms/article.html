This article by Fernando Buen Abad Domínguez originally appeared in the August 26, 2026 edition of Contralínea, a Mexican investigative magazine.

Here the cognitive war and its perverse commodity disguised as a bot farm appear laid bare. In capitalist societies, creators of the dictatorship of market competition and the subordination of consumers, every second of a gaze acquires economic and political value. In bot farms, what moves people, what enrages them; what provokes fear, what awakens desire, what generates permanence, is designed. The architecture of commercial digital communication thus finds a zone of convergence with political and military influence operations. A system designed to retain attention and dirty reality can be exploited by whoever seeks to retain, divert, or capture consciousness. The border between advertising, entertainment, propaganda, and psychological operation becomes porous. The subject believes they are consuming information while, simultaneously, becoming an exploitable resource and a victim of a thousand manipulations of class hatred.

A bot farm represents a peculiar factory. It does not produce objects we can store, transport, or touch. It produces atmospheres, the impression that something is everywhere, that an indignation is spontaneous, that a slogan expresses common sense, that a person is admired or detested by millions, that certain ideas have been left out of history. Its commodity is social perception. The raw material is human attention; the energy comes from digital infrastructures, labor, electricity, minerals, water, capital, and computing power; the final product is a modification of the field of the imaginable. Cognitive war thus turns subjectivity into strategic territory, and it begins when a person is made to confuse their own perception with the perception of their exploiters: factories of hatred.

That is the semiotic paradox worth placing at the center: the sign ceases to represent a shared reality and begins to fabricate the image of a shared reality. An artificial crowd can produce the sensation of a true crowd; that sensation modifies real behaviors; the real behaviors end up offering the evidence that seemed to justify the initial fiction. The circle closes with a disturbing efficiency: consensus is fabricated in order to later invoke it as proof. The bot does not need to have consciousness to take part in an operation aimed at modifying human consciousness. Its effectiveness comes precisely from that: it occupies the place of a social presence that never existed and, in doing so, alters the behavior of those who do exist. The conceptual inversion turns out to be decisive.

For centuries propaganda was understood as a message that tried to influence a population. Today we may be facing something more sophisticated: a machinery that first constructs the appearance of a population and then uses that appearance to influence real individuals. It is not merely a matter of saying “many people think this.” Thousands of signals are fabricated so that someone believes that many people think this. The recipient does not only receive an opinion; they receive a representation of others’ opinion. The manipulation penetrates a deeper level because it modifies the mirror in which each person imagines society.

That mechanism allows us to understand why cognitive war can be extraordinarily effective without achieving an argumentative victory. The objective is not always to convince; it may be to fatigue, fragment, disorient, ridicule, isolate, accelerate the emotional reaction, or destroy trust in any possibility of common knowledge. A convinced population still possesses a certainty around which it can organize; a population that considers it impossible to know what is true becomes much more available for resignation. Induced relativism can be as functional to power as affirmative propaganda.

The bot farm introduces a particular form of alienation: the person no longer faces only social products that appear as independent forces; they may also face a crowd that never existed. Let us imagine a worker who posts an opinion about labor conditions and receives hundreds of coordinated responses that present her as ignorant, dangerous, or ridiculous. She may believe she is facing a collective reaction. Perhaps it is twenty operators and thousands of automated accounts. The objective falsehood produces a true subjective effect: fear, self-censorship, isolation. The lie has managed to become experience.

That is one of the most refined forms of contemporary power: it does not need us to believe a complete falsehood; it needs us to act as if it were true. The gender perspective reveals a particularly severe dimension of this technology. Women who occupy public spaces can become targets of coordinated campaigns of sexualization, humiliation, threats, dissemination of manipulated images, and discredit. The violence acquires an exemplary function. The attack against a specific woman simultaneously communicates a message to many others: occupying public speech has a cost. The technology thus reproduces old structures of disciplining through unprecedented speeds. The aggression ceases to be a localized episode and can turn into a permanent cloud of harassment. The female body becomes a symbolic battlefield, while the automated dimension makes it possible to multiply the pressure without proportionally multiplying the aggressors.

And cognitive war can even intervene on perception so that the victims dispute among themselves while the structures that produce the harm disappear from the field of vision. That is why cognitive war must not be understood solely as a struggle between States, armies, or intelligence services. It also expresses a struggle for the ownership and control of the infrastructures capable of organizing collective perception. Whoever controls large volumes of data possesses an unprecedented cartography of desires, fears, habits, bonds, and vulnerabilities. Whoever can automate millions of interactions acquires an extraordinary capacity to experiment with populations. Society can become, without noticing it, a laboratory.

Instead of asking why so many people believe a certain thing, we ask what conditions made it possible for it to seem that so many people believe it. Instead of asking why an opinion went viral, we ask what infrastructure turned its circulation into an event. Instead of observing the user as the origin of the conversation, we look at the economic, technological, and labor relations that organize what the user is able to see. Suddenly, the isolated individual who seemed to make spontaneous decisions appears inserted into a machinery of visibility production. Freedom does not disappear, though the location where we must look for its limits changes. The true battle, then, does not occur only inside our heads.

It occurs around the material conditions that make it possible to produce what reaches our heads. This observation radically modifies the idea of sovereignty. A society can retain elections, parliaments, newspapers, and open networks while it progressively loses the capacity to autonomously establish which events acquire importance, which emotions dominate the debate, and which voices appear as the majority. Political sovereignty is eroded when perceptual sovereignty is outsourced. The revolution of consciousness requires recovering an elementary faculty that technological speed threatens to weaken: the right to stop.

Thinking slowly becomes a political practice. To contrast, contextualize, ask who wins, who loses, who owns the infrastructure, who pays the costs, which bodies receive the violence, which territories bear the extraction, which interests disappear behind an avalanche of messages: each question opens a crack in the appearance of spontaneity. The critical literacy of the 21st century cannot be limited to distinguishing a fake news item from a true one. It has to teach how to recognize who fabricates the stage where a truth can circulate and a lie can seem to be the majority.

Perhaps the most uncomfortable revelation is this: bot farms are not dangerous because machines are learning to seem human; they are dangerous because we human beings have built social relations where seeming to be a multitude can grant power similar to that of being a multitude. The fundamental problem is not inside the bot, it is in the society that has turned visibility into force, attention into commodity, and collective perception into disputable territory. When we understand this, the screen changes its meaning.

We no longer see simply opposing messages: we see relations of production, hierarchies, interests, bodies, territories, and resources disputing the possibility of defining what we will consider real. Cognitive war then ceases to seem a mysterious battle waged in some remote place on the internet and appears as a profoundly material struggle for social consciousness. Whoever manages to understand that inversion will have taken a decisive first step: to stop asking only who is deceiving us and to begin discovering who needs our own image of society to be falsified so that the existing order continues to seem inevitable.

Here appears the technified communicational crime. It is not reduced to a lie published on the internet. The isolated lie can be discussed, contrasted, refuted. The problem changes when the falsehood acquires infrastructure, repetition, speed, algorithmic segmentation, and the appearance of consensus. The operation consists of fabricating the psychological conditions for a statement to seem familiar before being examined. Repetition produces recognition; recognition disguises itself as evidence; the apparent evidence demands trust. Suddenly, a phrase that no one remembers having accepted begins to govern family conversations, electoral campaigns, markets, professional reputations, and collective conflicts. The fundamental crime may then be found elsewhere: in the industrial appropriation of the social capacity to produce meaning.

One of the most violent paradoxes of our time is that a machine can fabricate the appearance of a multitude until it gets the real multitude to end up obeying that appearance. The first semiotic inversion is there: we believe that signs represent society, when certain digital architectures begin to produce the society that those signs then seem to describe. An army of automated accounts can invent a trend; that trend can alter conversations, the conversations can modify perceptions, the perceptions can guide decisions and, finally, the decisions can be presented as a spontaneous expression of citizenship. Representation ceases to be a mirror and becomes a mold. The sign no longer arrives after the event: it prepares the conditions for the event to occur.

This apparently technical twist contains an extraordinary philosophical consequence: many times we are not before a manipulated opinion, but before a social reality previously designed so that certain opinions seem natural. Bot farms constitute an industrial form of that fabrication. Where we imagine dispersed users, individual desires, and unpredictable conversations, there may exist a coordinated infrastructure that administers identities, rhythms, emotions, slogans, silences, and conflicts. The traditional commodity needed a factory that transformed matter; the new industry of influence can transform attention into behavior. Each automated account functions as a small piece of a larger machinery whose product is not a visible object, but a mental climate. Uncertainty, indignation, enthusiasm, fear, suspicion, mockery are fabricated. The affective temperature of a population is administered. The screen seems to offer freedom of expression while an invisible architecture calculates which emotions should circulate at greater speed. Power thus reaches a particularly refined form: it no longer needs to directly order what people must think; it can intervene on the prior terrain where it is decided what deserves to be thought.

This perspective allows us to understand something even more unsettling. The bot does not have to convince everyone. It is enough for it to modify the perception each person has of others. If someone believes that thousands support a certain position, they may feel isolated in disagreeing. If a woman perceives that a wave of attacks against her is massive, she may abandon a public conversation, even if the attack comes from a few coordinated accounts. If a worker observes a digital avalanche blaming precarious individuals for an economic crisis, they may interpret a fabricated narrative as spontaneous consensus. The strategic objective does not consist only of persuading the individual: it consists of altering their representation of the collectivity. The other’s imagination about others is conquered. Therein lies one of the most profound semiotic operations of the era.

This observation modifies even our idea of freedom. For a long time we imagined communicational freedom as the possibility of emitting messages. Today we would have to add a more radical question: who has the means capable of deciding which messages acquire massive visibility? The formal freedom to speak can coexist with a gigantic inequality in the capacity to be heard. Millions can publish while a few actors, through automation, ad buying, data analysis, and algorithmic manipulation, can amplify certain discourses until turning them into ambience. Inequality ceases to appear only as a difference of income or property and begins to manifest as a difference of capacity to construct perceptible reality.

Here estrangement becomes necessary. It is worth looking at the networks in reverse: not asking first what people think, but who managed to make certain things seem thinkable; not asking only who spread a slogan, but who built the stage where that slogan acquired the appearance of a multitude; not observing only the content of the messages, but the material relations that determine their circulation. When we change the angle, the user ceases to appear as the absolute origin of communication and begins to reveal themselves as part of an economic, technological, and political architecture. The screen ceases to be a transparent window and becomes a machine for distributing possibilities.

And the revolution of consciousness begins precisely when we stop accepting as natural the social forms that were designed. Recognizing a bot farm does not consist only of detecting fake accounts. It means learning to suspect fabricated spontaneity, perfect unanimity, indignation that arrives synchronized, the trend that seems to emerge from nowhere, the emotion that immediately finds thousands of echoes. It means recovering an elementary political faculty: distinguishing between what a society feels and what an industry of influence manages to make it feel about what others supposedly feel.

Thus, the challenge does not consist of rejecting technology, because that way out would be as naive as blaming the printing press for all the pamphlets of history. The problem is in the social relations that organize technology, in who owns its infrastructures, who decides its rules, who profits from the capture of attention, and who pays the human, environmental, and democratic costs. The decisive question then ceases to be “can robots deceive us?” and becomes another, much more uncomfortable: “what kind of society needs to fabricate artificial multitudes to preserve the appearance of consensus?” When we formulate that question, the bot loses its status as an isolated monster and appears as a symptom. We no longer contemplate a digital anomaly, we contemplate a social structure that learned to produce reality through automated signs.

Perhaps therein lies the most profound revelation: bot farms do not represent only a falsification of public conversation; they represent the attempt to turn public conversation into an administrable commodity. The decisive struggle then occurs around something apparently intangible: the collective possibility of naming the world. Whoever administers that possibility can alter the perception of conflict, invert responsibilities, render labor invisible, discipline bodies, accelerate consumption, and turn ecological devastation into a remote datum. Recovering critical consciousness implies breaking that spell, understanding that each trend has a material history, that each consensus has conditions of production, and that each collective emotion can be disputed. Communicational emancipation begins when we stop asking what message we should consume and begin to ask who built the conditions for that message to seem inevitable. In that instant, the screen ceases to dictate the world: it becomes an object within the world again, and we recover the capacity to transform it.

Fernando Buen Abad Domínguez holds a doctorate in philosophy.
