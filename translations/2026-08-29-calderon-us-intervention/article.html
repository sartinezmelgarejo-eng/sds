This article by Nancy Flores originally appeared in the August 29, 2026 edition of Contralínea, an independent Mexican investigative magazine.

The supposed "war" on drug trafficking that Felipe Calderón Hinojosa launched in an attempt to legitimize his government —and which was later revealed to be a war to position the Sinaloa Cartel, given its partnership with Genaro García Luna— brought about the greatest intervention by the United States government in Mexico's recent history.

The guiding axis of that intervention was the Mérida Initiative. Under a scheme of supposed cooperation, the neighboring country to the north took control of operational, military, and intelligence strategies. Thus, during the governments of George Bush and Barack Obama, members of agencies such as the DEA, the FBI, and the CIA participated openly and covertly in field operations, and gained access to national security information through their participation in Plataforma México.

Declassified documents from the Embassy, the State Department, and the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) reveal that, during the Calderón six-year term, U.S. institutions took advantage of the government's weakness to impose their own law, and to systematically violate Mexican sovereignty and independence.

They also detail that, with U.S. sponsorship, Mexico gained access to technology related to espionage work and the mass interception of communications, although the references to it indicate that the use of that software would be carried out with court orders.

In a monthly narcotics report that the U.S. Embassy partially declassified —and which it sent, on December 15, 2009, to various authorities of the neighboring country— reference is made to the judicial assistance being provided to our country under the framework of the Mérida Initiative, although —due to the censorship of the document— it is not possible to know further details: "…to assist these agencies in carrying out judicially authorized wiretaps."

In that same document it is observed that all of Mexico's security institutions were part of the U.S. strategy. For example, it is cited that in that month of December 2009 the "acquisition of polygraph equipment worth 2.5 million dollars to improve the capabilities in this area of CISEN, the SSP, the SAT, the INAMI, and the PGR" was underway.

At that time, in 2009, the director of the Center for Investigation and National Security (Cisen) was Guillermo Valdés Castellanos; the head of the Ministry of Public Security was García Luna; at the helm of the Tax Administration Service was the now-retired justice Alfredo Gutiérrez Ortiz Mena; the National Migration Institute was led by Cecilia Romero Castillo; and at the PGR was Arturo Chávez Chávez, who had recently replaced Eduardo Medina Mora.

Disguised as a close relationship between the Mexican government and U.S. authorities, the interference in security matters is laid bare in that same report by the U.S. Embassy. In it, Mexico's actions and strategies on public security and the supposed fight against organized crime are detailed, but it also reveals the crisis of violence that was already being experienced halfway through that six-year term.

The communication, still under the charge of then-ambassador Carlos Pascual, indicated that on October 28, 2009, high-level bilateral meetings were held in Washington, in which strategies to dismantle the drug-trafficking cartels and to create "a 21st-century border" were addressed.

In addition, it notes that on November 4 of that same year another bilateral meeting was held for the implementation of the Mérida Initiative, at the facilities of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. "In addition to informing the representatives of the U.S. government and the Mexican government about the status of each project, the Mexican government presented details about the new database and project-tracking system that would be implemented at the end of November."

By November 18, once again, high-ranking officials from both countries "met to address pillars II (institutional strengthening) and IV (building resilient communities) of the new bilateral Mérida strategy. Both sides analyzed joint programs and actions on internal controls ('confidence control'), strengthening of state and local capacities, human rights, drug-demand reduction, socioeconomic factors linked to delinquency and crime, and judicial reform."

Regarding U.S. investments in this area, it indicates that "on November 27 the purchase order was signed for 19 trucks equipped with mobile vehicle and cargo inspection systems using gamma radiation (VACIS) for the SSP (18 units) and the Sedena (1 unit), using Mérida Initiative funds."

Likewise, it specifies a series of training and instruction activities carried out on U.S. territory for Mexican personnel from different corporations. For example, it cites that: "on November 20, 2009, 25 members of the SSP's classification personnel [under García Luna] completed six weeks of training in Colorado. The training focused on the classification and reintegration process, and helped participants develop procedures and instruments for the Mexican classification system. The participants also received a train-the-trainer course. The third course for prison instructors continues to be taught at the New Mexico Corrections Academy."

The report also gives an account of an increase in violence and the human rights crisis in which Mexico was already immersed. Under the subtitle "Increase in violence," the report details that "at the end of the third year of the Calderón administration and a few days before the third anniversary of the war against drug-trafficking organizations (DTOs), the media counted close to 16,000 victims related to organized crime. November registered a decline in homicides, with approximately 682 deaths; a marked reduction compared to the previous five months, though close to the 2009 average."

It adds that in that month "high-impact crimes continued, including the murder of the Secretary of Public Security of García, Nuevo León —retired brigadier general Juan Arturo Esparza— which occurred four days after his appointment. In the neighboring municipality of Escobedo, Nuevo León, the Secretary of Public Security resigned after barely a few hours in office due to acts of intimidation. More than half of the 51 municipal Secretary of Security posts in the state of Nuevo León remain vacant. The states of Zacatecas, Morelos, Guerrero, and Durango reported direct attacks against law-enforcement facilities, most carried out with grenades and high-powered automatic weapons. Likewise, coordinated retaliatory attacks were recorded against eight facilities of the PGR (Attorney General's Office) and the municipal police following a high-profile arrest made on November 20 in Celaya; those attacks recalled those that occurred on July 11 in Michoacán."

Regarding public perception of insecurity, the report indicates that a national survey "revealed an increase in citizen frustration regarding the current security strategy. In a speech marking this milestone, the president highlighted the achievements attained to date and noted that progress had been made in strengthening institutions. In subsequent interviews, Calderón acknowledged that public perception represents one of his greatest challenges."

Awarded by the DEA as one of the supposedly "best" police officers in Mexico, Genaro García Luna was a key piece in Calderón's six-year term. Today imprisoned in the United States for his partnership with the Sinaloa Cartel, the former Secretary of Public Security of that government directly led the "war" strategy.

In that November 2009 report prepared by the U.S. Embassy, the reference to the inauguration of García Luna's bunker stands out precisely —from where Plataforma México was officially operated with Mérida Initiative financing, but where, unofficially, an illegal policy of mass interception of communications and espionage was carried out.

In this regard, the monthly report details that "Ambassador Pascual led the U.S. Embassy team at the inauguration ceremony of the 'Bunker' on November 24, 2009. Some 500 guests attended, among them the president of Mexico, other distinguished officials of the Mexican government, and prominent businessmen of the country. The facilities, of 7,000 square meters, will serve as the information operations center of 'Plataforma México,' connecting more than 600 points throughout the national territory, including 169 Federal Police stations."

Regarding U.S. cooperation, it notes that "the software technologies provided by the United States government will allow the SSP to develop a new, uniform, and standardized 'National Response Framework' for coordination and communication among multiple agencies, in order to protect the population and perform critical functions immediately after a disaster."

Likewise, it states that "the objective of the assistance sponsored by the United States government is to endow the [Mexican] SSP with standardized core capabilities (similar to those of the DHS [Department of Homeland Security], DOJ [Department of Justice], and FEMA [Federal Emergency Management Agency] agencies) in incident-management processes, protocols, law-enforcement procedures, and other operational and information functions. The center will allow the SSP to respond quickly and effectively through consistent procedures, a common incident language, and standardized communication protocols, in addition to ensuring a shared understanding of the situation (common situational awareness) and of the operational picture from the national command center."

According to the Embassy, the center they were leaving in García Luna's hands placed "Mexico ahead of other developed countries in terms of emergency preparedness and police action 'during and after' incidents of various kinds (whether natural or man-made), covering all affected geographic areas and law-enforcement bodies at the national level."
