This editorial originally appeared in the September 2, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left-wing daily newspaper.

In presenting her second State of the Nation Report, President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo offered verifiable data illustrating the fulfillment of the commitments she made to the people of Mexico and the soundness of the national project she is advancing. Perhaps the most significant advance for citizens is the 51 percent decrease in intentional homicides and the generalized drop in high-impact crimes. Although acceptable levels of security are still far off, it is undeniable that today the lives and property of residents are better protected than two years ago, and much better than they were at the end of 2018.

The figures also speak of change in other areas. The minimum wage has recovered by 154 percent in real terms since 2018, and for the first time so-called app workers enjoy labor rights. Foreign direct investment is at record levels; the unemployment rate is just 2.7 percent (in the United States, for reference, the corresponding figure is 4.1); inflation is now within the central bank's target range; the peso has appreciated for a second consecutive six-year term; and tourism is booming, with 51 million international travelers in the first half of the year.

The Well-Being Programs mobilize one trillion pesos that reach 42,860,296 beneficiaries. All public-school students—from primary school through upper-secondary—receive scholarships, and there is an intention to extend this support to those pursuing university studies. This year, 156,000 new places will have been created in upper-secondary education. The Rosario Castellanos National University will have 13 new academic campuses across the country, with 119,280 students. The Benito Juárez Well-Being Universities already have 285,000 students on 239 campuses.

The President set as a six-year goal the addition of 9,000 beds to the health system, more than double those added in 36 years of neoliberalism. Some 90,000 homes have already been allocated to families earning less than 2.9 times the minimum wage; 278,000 more are under construction, and the aim is to deliver 1.8 million. Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples were recognized as subjects of public law in the second article of the Constitution, a change that is not merely symbolic: in two years, 25 billion pesos have been delivered to the communities so that they may decide how to use them through their own forms of government. The reconstruction of the passenger rail network, a “hallmark of the Fourth Transformation of public life,” is advancing.

It is clear that every ruler seeks to highlight the strengths of their administration and chooses the indicators that most favor them. Nevertheless, President Sheinbaum's Report reflects much more than numbers: in its emphases, its evocations, its choice of words, and the audiences of its message, it coherently conveys a central idea: the consolidation of a project that belongs neither to one person nor to one group, but to society as a whole. In speaking of republican austerity, of the separation between political and economic power, in embracing the maxim that “for the good of all, the poor come first,” or in noting that she has visited all 32 states of the country, the President not only reaffirms the continuity she offered when she asked for people's votes, but also reminds supporters and opponents alike that Mexico is undergoing a paradigm shift, and that the dogmas of the free market have given way to a social economy in which justice is not an abstraction but a right guaranteed by the State.

One sentence sums up the reversal in the conception of public service: “we cannot expect the benefits of growth to reach every family on their own; the State has a guiding responsibility: to plan and promote development, to guarantee social rights, to strengthen a fair distribution of wealth.” Indeed, it has been shown that, left to their own devices, market mechanisms enrich a few at the expense of the majority, and upholding human rights is no longer understood as a handout from the State, but as “a way of recognizing the dignity and effort of millions of Mexican women and men.”
