This article by Obed Rosas originally appeared in the September 6, 2026 edition of SinEmbargo, one of Mexico's leading independent digital news outlets.

Mexico City, September 6 (SinEmbargo).— In The Lord of the Rings, Sauron uses a palantír, a magical stone, as a tool of surveillance, communication, and psychological manipulation. With it he watches from afar and exploits the fear and desperation of his adversaries. Inspired by the seeing-stones of J. R. R. Tolkien's epic saga, the ultra-right-wing tech magnate Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, and other co-founders named their software company Palantir, "the most valuable military contractor in the United States that makes neither bombs nor weapons," as The New York Times defines it.

Palantir "was really the first company to be completely anti-woke," Alex Karp declared this past November during the presentation of the third-quarter 2025 results. At that same event, Karp asserted that woke thinking was a "fundamental risk" for Palantir, the United States, and the world, and described it as "a regressive way of thinking that is corrupting and weakening our institutions."

Just a few years ago, Alex Karp boasted of his support for Joe Biden and Kamala Harris. He called himself progressive. That changed with Donald Trump's return to the Presidency. The journalist Michael Steinberger recounted in his book The Philosopher of the Valley how the October 7 attacks in Israel consolidated Karp's "political metamorphosis," and how the anti-Israel protests that followed "led him to break definitively with the Democrats." "They do nothing about immigration or Iran. They talk constantly about racism, but they don't talk about antisemitism," Karp told Steinberger.

Unlike Karp, the magnate Peter Thiel occupies a privileged place in Trumpism. A co-founder of PayPal, a tech investor, and one of the most influential figures in Silicon Valley, Thiel has managed to combine his fortune with technology, his political philosophy, and the capacity to intervene directly in the formation of new cadres, as in the case of Vice President JD Vance, one of the possible political heirs of the MAGA movement.

Vance met Thiel in 2011, when he was studying law at Yale and attended a lecture by the businessman. Years later, Vance himself wrote that that talk had been "the most significant moment" of his time at Yale. Since then, he has become a disciple of Thiel, who is also one of his donors. In the last U.S. presidential race, he spoke at the Republican National Convention, publicly backed Trump, and contributed 1.25 million dollars to his campaign.

In June 2021, ProPublica revealed how Thiel and his partners founded Palantir thanks to an initial investment from In-Q-Tel, a venture capital fund backed by the CIA. Since then, Palantir has obtained federal contracts from the U.S. military to search for terrorists and from Immigration and Customs Enforcement to locate undocumented immigrants. It also has contracts with the Israeli military, which has provoked numerous protests in several countries. In recent months, moreover, it has drawn closer to the right-wing governments of Argentina and Ecuador.

The New York Times explains that Palantir is a software and data-analysis company that uses technologies such as data mining and more recent ones such as artificial intelligence for national-security purposes. It notes that the company has benefited enormously from the euphoria surrounding AI in the stock market.

For its part, The Economist explained in August 2025 that this company helps all kinds of clients—from intelligence services to fast-food chains—analyze their data better and, therefore, improve their operations. Its dizzying recent growth is due, in large part, to the enthusiasm for adopting AI for these purposes. "Palantir's competitive advantage comes not only from its software and its highly trained engineers, but also from a high-level security clearance that allows it to process classified information from U.S. defense and intelligence agencies. This grants it a competitive advantage that lets it fend off its competitors."

Wired, for its part, qualified that what Palantir sells them is not just software, but the idea of a comprehensive, almost magical solution to complex problems. To that end, it notes, Palantir tends to use the language and aesthetics of war, presenting itself as a powerful quasi-military intelligence partner. "Palantir is here to revolutionize the sector and turn the institutions we partner with into the best in the world," Karp himself asserted on a February 2025 earnings call. "And, when necessary, to intimidate our enemies and, on occasion, eliminate them."

"Palantir sends its employees to work inside its clients' organizations, essentially as consultants, helping them customize their data flows, troubleshoot problems, and fix errors. It calls these workers 'forward-deployed software engineers,' a term that appears to be inspired by the concept of forward-deployed troops, who are stationed in adversarial regions to deter nearby enemies from attacking," Wired elaborated.

A former Palantir employee told the outlet that the company also uses code words for certain jobs, such as "Delta" for a forward-deployed engineer and "Echo" for Palantir's version of a product manager. According to him, these code words come from the NATO phonetic alphabet, a set of code words intended for use over military radio.

Palantir's expansion in Latin America has begun to take shape through approaches to governments in various countries. Between this past April and May, officials from Argentina, Chile, and Paraguay held closed-door meetings with Peter Thiel. These meetings opened up questions about the risks to human rights, digital sovereignty, and control of data in the region.

The most advanced case is Ecuador. Derechos Digitales América Latina notes that in January 2025 Daniel Noboa's government incorporated Palantir into its digital transformation plan, and that in May of that year it formalized a collaboration with the company to combat customs fraud. Later, after a meeting between Noboa and Alex Karp at the Davos Forum, it was announced that Palantir would open its first office in Latin America in Ecuador. The collaboration also extended to the fight against illegal mining and fishing and to preventing fraud in public-sector loans. However, the content of the contracts is not publicly known.

In Argentina, it was reported that Peter Thiel turned Buenos Aires into his base of operations for the Southern Cone. The businessman met behind closed doors with Javier Milei and members of his cabinet, and also traveled to Paraguay, where he met with President Santiago Peña, and to Chile, where he held meetings with libertarian and neoliberal figures.

This past May, El País revealed how Thiel landed in Argentina with his husband, Matt Danzeisen (a former BlackRock executive), and their children, and bought a 12-million-dollar mansion in Barrio Parque, one of the most exclusive neighborhoods in Buenos Aires. In May 2024, the newspaper notes, he had already visited Javier Milei at the Casa Rosada, in a meeting that went almost unnoticed.

The Chilean press reported, in turn, that Thiel met in April with Chilean President José Antonio Kast at the La Moneda Palace, in a meeting handled under wraps that drew criticism from the opposition. The same report picks up on Palantir's regional approach through Ecuador, where the company maintains a contract with Noboa's government to combat customs crimes.

ElDiario.es places Palantir within a broader process of technological expansion by the new Latin American right. The outlet notes that in Ecuador the company has already opened an office and has a contract with Noboa's customs agency; in Argentina there is so far only an approach, while in Colombia the scenario is still a possibility. For ElDiario.es, the pattern beginning to emerge is that different governments in the region end up turning to the same data-analysis and surveillance company.

This past April 18, Palantir's X account published an excerpt from the book The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West, by Alexander C. Karp and Nicholas W. Zamiska, in which it laid out 22 points, in the manner of a manifesto, to explain why the Silicon Valley elite has an obligation to participate in the defense of the nation.

"The limits of soft power, of lofty rhetoric alone, have been exposed. The ability of free and democratic societies to prevail requires more than moral appeal. It requires hard power, and hard power in this century will be built on software," reads one of the points.

In another, it warns that the question is not whether AI weapons will be built, but "who will build them and for what purpose." In that sense, it elaborates: "Our adversaries will not pause to indulge in theatrical debates about the merits of developing technologies with critical military and national-security applications. They will proceed."

Among other ideas, it states that "we should, as a society, seriously consider moving away from an all-volunteer force and fight the next war only if everyone shares the risk and the cost," and it warns that "if a U.S. Marine asks for a better rifle, we should build it; and the same applies to software."

It also maintains that no other country in the history of the world has done more to advance progressive values than the United States, and it asserts that "American power has made possible an extraordinarily long peace."

"Too many have forgotten, or perhaps take for granted, that nearly a century of some version of peace has prevailed in the world without a great-power military conflict. At least three generations—billions of people and their children and now grandchildren—have never known a world war," it adds.

Finally, it maintains that one must resist "the superficial temptation of an empty, hollow pluralism. We, in America and more broadly in the West, have resisted for the past half-century defining national cultures in the name of inclusivity. But inclusion in what?"
