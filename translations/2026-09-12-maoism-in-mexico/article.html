This article by Roberto Rico Ramírez originally appeared in the September 12, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left-wing daily newspaper.

On the occasion of the publication of the book Estrella Roja en México and 50 years after the death of Mao Zedong (September 9, 1976), Luis Hernández Navarro reminds us of the presence in Mexico of the Chinese revolution, which left a deep mark on our country. The intelligentsia of those days—beginning with Vicente Lombardo, on through Fernando Benítez and Lázaro Cárdenas—were astonished by the Asian giant.

The book is a mirror of the trajectory and social practice of the one who writes it; I first remember him at that gathering we held in Monterrey with the Coordinadora Línea de Masas. He has always been a social fighter; his books and articles reflect his orientation toward the mass movement, such as Chiapas: La guerra y la paz; No habrá recreo. Contra reforma constitucional y desobediencia magisterial; and Siembra de concreto, cosecha de ira; Hermanos en armas. La hora de los policías comunitarios y las autodefensas—six books with the Brigada para Leer en Libertad; in the Vientos del Pueblo series, La primavera magisterial; and in the popular collection, La pintura en la pared, the latter two published by the Fondo de Cultura Económica, among others.

Luis carries out a surgical dissection of the great social movements and their militant apparatuses, of their struggles waged in the heat of social battles and their combination with the struggles for local power. Luis Hernández, at the same time that he details the actions of the militants, lays the ground for mentioning the struggles these subjects wage. He has the skill and the fine touch to carry out exhaustive research and to weave the narrative and—the extraordinary part—the use of narrative to tell history and the stories of its protagonists; narrative is an art he nimbly develops, reconstructing the social actor, leading us to reflection and to the development of critical thinking about the event itself. I remember the author as a social fighter when he was general secretary of the union section of the Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia and in the CNTE; he too put himself on the line many times in marches, strikes, and actions that he reflects in his talent as a writer. In those days there were countless Maoist groups in the country that operated clandestinely in the face of the State's repressive forces. Some risked their lives in the guerrilla movement, others in the social movement, but acting through clandestine cells, all of them pushing forward the processes of building socialism in Mexico from different approaches.

There we have leaders such as Camilo Chávez, Edelmiro Maldonado, Esther Chapa, Vicente Estrada, Amador Tobón, Joel Aquino, José Santos, Judith Reyes, Antonio Martínez, Salvador Zarco, Paco Taibo II, among others, who bet on the construction of powerful, autonomous, grassroots popular mass organizations, of combative student and union contingents; some placed at the center a column of cadres. All of them historical subjects who created their own utopia, which still permeates the Mexican left, with its diversity, complementarity, and complexity. The only peasant and popular mass organizations that still persist in the Mexican left are those created by the Mexican Maoists: the CNTE, the MUP, the CNPA; countless neighborhood, community, ejido, and autonomous organizations that still go on creating communitarian ideas of decision-making with their paradigms, drawing experiences from their cultural roots; there we have the forms of organization of the EZLN, sowing their own paths in Chiapas as an example. At the center of any movement there are always the social subjects who make the revolutionary cultural determinant possible.

The Chinese people and Mao Zedong have always been present in the Mexican left. As the author rightly puts it: "Not all who belong are here, but there are some who allow us to understand key moments of our recent history." Let us look forward to its presentation at the FIL-Zócalo.
