What's the foundational human right? The right to work and to make or grow as much as you need to live with dignity and security. From that right, other rights follow—to housing, to food, to mobility and so on.

In 1994, the NAFTA free trade agreement—negotiated in a neoliberal framework—took away that basic natural right in favor of corporate rights. It was foreign corporations that got the freedom to migrate, resulting in the many human rights violations we suffer today. In Mexico, we've seen displacement and worker migration, water and air pollution, and loss of access to safe drinking water; in the US, we've lost industrial jobs, which has often resulted in depressed workers with increased suicides and drug abuse, in turn creating a market for drugs with the rise of the drug cartels and expanding gun trade.

In 2018, the USMCA, or T-MEC, continued that agreement, with US unions demanding and getting some improvements for workers. The new Labor Accords specifically supported the right of workers to form democratic, independent unions in Mexico, giving workers the hope of getting rid of corrupt charro unions and gaining the power to win better wages and working conditions. In 2019, Mexican labor reform put that right into law.

Workers did begin to organize independent unions. But companies never wanted to enforce the labor reforms of 2019—so repression and violence against the labor organizers continued; it simply took a different form. Under the PRI/PAN governments, the state perpetrated the repression and persecution of union leaders, including firing on demonstrators. That's no longer true. Now, the companies hire thugs, some connected to cartels, to intimidate workers.

But even though the United Nations and the International Labor Organization recognize labor rights as human rights, Mexico does not.

The new report to Mexico's Comisión Nacional de los Derechos Humanos (CNDH) makes a strong case for changing that and for new protocols to protect those who are exercising their right to form unions.
