This article by José Shaddai Olvera Torres originally appeared in the September 28, 2026 edition of El Chamuco, a Mexican satirical political magazine.

Larry Rubin, the US Republican Party's representative in Mexico and president of the American Society of Mexico, has called on Mexican senators to reject the constitutional reform promoted by President Claudia Sheinbaum that seeks to establish sole Mexican nationality as a requirement for anyone aspiring to the Presidency of the Republic, the governorships, and the head of government of Mexico City. Rubin questioned restricting access to those offices to people who hold a single nationality.

Through social media, Rubin asked the 128 senators to weigh their responsibility to citizens before voting on the initiative. "Each senator will have to decide whether their responsibility lies first with a party instruction or with the Mexicans they swore to represent," he wrote.

The statement comes after the Chamber of Deputies approved the reform with 334 votes in favor, 109 against, and one abstention. The bill was sent to the Senate, where debate and a vote are expected.

The reform proposes amending Articles 82, 116, and 122 of the Constitution to establish that those who aspire to the Presidency, the governorships, or the head of government of Mexico City maintain an exclusive legal and political bond with the Mexican State. The bill provides that anyone holding another nationality must renounce it before registering as a candidate and demonstrate that the legal tie to the other country has ceased to exist under that country's laws. The measure would begin to apply starting with the 2028 electoral process, so it would have no effect on the 2027 elections.

Rubin questioned that holding a second nationality could be used as a criterion for determining a person's loyalty to Mexico.

Dual nationality does not mean dual loyalty. Being Mexican is not measured by how many passports we hold; it is measured by our commitment to Mexico.

The Republican representative also said the reform could create a division between citizens with one and with two nationalities, while calling for consideration of binational families and of Mexicans who maintain ties to the United States.

It is not the first time Rubin has questioned the proposal. Earlier in September he called the initiative a "very serious mistake" and maintained that holding more than one passport does not determine a person's loyalty to Mexico. On that occasion he claimed the measure could affect millions of Mexicans with dual nationality; the figure of potentially affected people that Rubin has cited has varied and should not be presented as an official number without a demographic source to back it up.

Sheinbaum, for her part, has defended the reform on the grounds that those who occupy the Presidency, a governorship, or the head of government should maintain an exclusive legal and political bond with Mexico. The president has held that no head of state should have "two flags."

The bill is now in the Senate of the Republic, where it needs a qualified majority to amend the Constitution. The debate is also taking place amid differences among the parliamentary groups allied with Morena; according to recent reports, the ruling bloc and its allies have only a narrow margin to reach the required votes.

Rubin's appeal thus adds to the public debate over the nationality requirements for access to the country's main executive offices, as the Senate prepares to decide the future of the reform.
