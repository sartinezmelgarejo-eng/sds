This article by Pablo Moctezuma Barragán originally appeared in the October 2, 2026 edition of Contralínea, a Mexican investigative journalism magazine.

Part two. The use of "spirituality" in the earthly world and in the structures of government serves selfish interests that, in practice, contradict even the religious discourse of the dominators.

To control the population, prohibitions, restrictions and sacrifices are imposed on it through rules so strict that they serve as a pretext to punish not those who violate religious dogmas, but those who affect the economic and political interests of the elites. The population is criminalized and its "faults" are used as justification to put the rebel to death.

In the era of the Inquisition, people could be imprisoned, tortured or executed, even burned at the stake. Since the 19th century, the "civil death" of the rebel has also been promoted through smear campaigns in the press. Today, well into the 21st century, these practices extend to social media, where lynchings by bots are the order of the day.

Conservative interests, aimed at preserving privileges and driving regression, always counted on the active support of the high religious hierarchies. Pius IX opposed the first Reform Laws, which he refused to recognize and disqualified. The pope stated: "We raise our pontifical voice with apostolic freedom in this your full assembly to condemn, reprove and declare null and without any value the aforementioned decrees."

The conservative directorate used the newspapers La Sociedad and La Patria to foment hatred against the government, slander it and discredit its project of drafting a new Constitution in keeping with the times.

Despite resorting to religious arguments, accusing the liberals of being impious and issuing decrees of excommunication in the churches and on street corners, the conservatives could not prevent the approval of the Federal Constitution of the United Mexican States of 1857. Then, to overthrow the constitutional order and maintain the political and economic privileges of the Church, they staged a coup d'état through the Plan of Tacubaya and unleashed the Reform War, also known as the Three Years' War.

Not satisfied with their defeat, they brought about the French invasion. The supreme pontiff and the local high ecclesiastical hierarchy opposed the liberal constitutional order and backed the conservative cause.

While Pius IX condemned the liberal reforms, the Mexican bishops prohibited swearing allegiance to the Constitution of 1857 and denied absolution to those who had done so without retracting. Under the slogan "Religion and Fueros," the conservatives demanded preserving the special ecclesiastical courts and keeping the Catholic religion as the only official one; in addition, they sought to impose a foreign prince.

Through the encyclical Quanta Cura and the Syllabus errorum (catalog of errors), published in 1864, Pius IX condemned liberalism and the separation of Church and State. Likewise, he backed the monarchist project sustained by the French intervention. Maximilian was received by the pope in the Vatican after accepting the crown and before traveling to Mexico.

On the occasion of the entry of Maximilian and Carlota into the capital, the archbishops Pelagio Antonio de Labastida y Dávalos, of Mexico, and Clemente de Jesús Munguía, of Michoacán, signed, together with other prelates, a pastoral letter welcoming the new "sovereign."

In the 20th century, Efraín González Luna, ideologue and founder of the National Action Party (PAN) together with Manuel Gómez Morín, justified the Church's struggle against the constituted power. He argued that it had defended itself from the plundering of its patrimony and had fulfilled its primordial task of guiding and organizing the people. Likewise, he maintained that Catholic society could not fail to react –sometimes with undoubted vigor– to the aggression of power.

According to González Luna, that society even achieved victories that allowed it to expel the agents of subversion from positions of command and to occupy those posts for some time (González Luna, 1954: 40). It is worth stressing that among those agents of subversion was Benito Juárez, while those who occupied the positions of command were conservatives of the ilk of Miguel Miramón and Félix María Zuloaga.

In 19th-century Mexico, Porfirio Díaz rose up in arms against Benito Juárez and, later, against Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada. Once in the presidency, he returned to the Church a large part of its political and economic power, to the point that the bishop of San Luis Potosí, Ignacio Montes de Oca y Obregón, came to affirm jubilantly in 1900: "The Reform Laws are extinguished logs." The Catholic press also contributed to supporting the regime through newspapers such as El Tiempo, El País and La Voz de la Verdad, which praised the dictator Díaz.

After the intense revolutionary struggle of the Mexican people, the bishops condemned the provisions of the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States of 1917 that they considered contrary to the rights and doctrine of the Church.

On June 15, 1917, Pope Benedict XV backed the bishops' protest and described it as "a thing very much in conformity with the pastoral office and most worthy of our praise." For his part, the archbishop of Guadalajara, Francisco Orozco y Jiménez, disqualified the Constitution for its "tendency destructive of religion, of culture and of traditions."

Years later, on February 3, 1926, the archbishop of Mexico, José Mora y del Río, made the following statements, as published by El Universal the next day:

"The doctrine of the Church is invariable, because it is divinely revealed truth. The protest that we Mexican prelates formulated against the Constitution of 1917 stands firm. It has not been modified but strengthened, because it derives from the doctrine of the Church [which] will undertake a campaign against unjust laws contrary to natural law. The Episcopate, clergy and Catholics do not recognize and will combat articles 3, 5, 27 and 130 of the Constitution in force. We cannot, for any reason, vary this criterion without betraying our faith and our religion" (El Universal, February 4, 1926).

In June 1926, the reform to the Penal Code known as the Calles Law was issued, published on July 2 of that year, which established sanctions for crimes and offenses in matters of religious worship and external discipline.

Just as the Church –under the religious mantle– intervened improperly in politics, condemned the Constitution and defended economic and political interests to preserve its earthly power, the Calles government imposed restrictions on worship and religious practices, whose exercise corresponds to each person according to their conscience.

The intransigence and violence unleashed during the Cristiada were also fostered by the government of Plutarco Elías Calles. In those years, Manuel Gómez Morín, who would later be one of the founders of the PAN, presided over the Board of Directors of the Bank of Mexico.

On November 18, 1926, Pope Pius XI issued the encyclical Iniquis afflictisque, on the persecution of the Church in Mexico. The pontifical letter stoked the passions of Mexican Catholics. Among other things, it said:

"It is true that in the first times of the Church and in later times Christians have been treated atrociously, but perhaps it has never happened in any place or time that a small number of men, trampling and violating the rights of God and of the Church, without any regard for past glories, without any feeling of piety toward their fellow citizens, totally shackled the freedom of the majority in such premeditated cunning masked with the appearance of laws. [That] which they call the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States [was the work of those who] possessed by a blind fury [wanted] to harm the Church in every possible way."

The encyclical was published when the uprisings of the Cristero War, which lasted until 1929, had already begun. Later, in Acerba animi, of 1932, Pius XI denounced the situation of the Church and of Catholics in Mexico, while defending the decision to resume public worship after the agreements of 1929.

In Firmissimam constantiam, of 1937, he recognized, under certain conditions, the legitimacy of resistance against abuses of power, without excluding violent defense, provided that the means were licit, proportional and did not cause greater harm than what was sought to be remedied. However, he excluded that defense from the tasks of the clergy and of Catholic Action, and centered his message on religious formation, the organization of the faithful and the call for peace and unity.

Decades later, in 1974, the federal government sent the architect Pedro Moctezuma Díaz Infante to meet with Pope Paul VI to improve Mexico's relations with the Vatican and invite him to visit the country. The pontiff received him with his back turned and, after having him sit in front of him, with a narrow table in between, reproached him that Mexican laws contravened those of the Catholic Church and demanded that he reform articles 3, 5, 27 and 130 of the Constitution. He declined the invitation for reasons of "health."

During the consolidation of neoliberalism, the reactionary government of Carlos Salinas de Gortari officially reestablished diplomatic relations between Mexico and the Holy See on September 21, 1992. This "achievement" was obtained after the reforms to constitutional articles 3, 5, 24, 27 and 130 and the promulgation of the Law of Religious Associations and Public Worship, in July of that year. Thus the old demands of the Church in its dispute with the Mexican State were met.

The religious right has been a minority sector that seeks to impose the interests of a few, control the population and appropriate the wealth it generates. To subjugate the majority, it uses spirituality as a justification for its domination and for the violence with which it imposes it, and presents economic, political and cultural conflicts as religious disputes.

Not all Catholics nor all members of the clergy have promoted privileges and exploitation. Benito Juárez declared himself Catholic and defended the separation of Church and State, while relentlessly combating the spurious interests of the clergy.

Bartolomé de las Casas openly denounced the crimes of the Spanish invaders; Vasco de Quiroga promoted economic autonomy and respect for Indigenous peoples; Francisco Javier Clavijero vindicated Mexican culture and identity, and thus contributed to anticolonial thought.

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz questioned the subordination of women in colonial society; Fray Servando Teresa de Mier, Miguel Hidalgo and José María Morelos promoted national independence and social justice. For his part, Celedonio Domeco de Jarauta was a Spanish priest who heroically fought the US invaders and who was later captured and executed by order of the conservative general Anastasio Bustamante.

Spirituality plays an important role in the lives of people and societies, but it can promote both liberation and oppression. The problem does not lie in the religious condition, but in the use of faith for purposes of domination, particularly by the high ecclesiastical hierarchy.

To oppress, the conservatives use the power of institutions that had a liberating origin. It must not be forgotten that Christianity arose among the sectors subjected to the slaveholding imperial order, but its institutions were later used by the dominant elites to enslave and colonize.

The large property owners impose their values and take advantage of the religious sentiment of the population to preserve a system that sacrifices the majority and concentrates property in the hands of the elite.

To achieve this, they dictate norms so strict and inhumane that they are impracticable. Even the promoters of religious dogma violate their own precepts, but they enjoy impunity as long as they remain aligned with the great interests. Thus a double standard is installed: one belief is preached and the opposite is lived. The rules then serve to punish those who challenge the dominant power and to accustom the majority to living with a permanent feeling of guilt.

The conservatives say they defend life and the family, but the norms they promote seek to keep women subordinated to a patriarchal society, without power of decision even over their own bodies.

The defense of indissoluble marriage and of exclusively reproductive sexual relations reinforces that control and contributes to preserving a social order based on property and inheritance. Added to this is the imposition of priestly celibacy as a mechanism of discipline over the intimate life of the clergy.

The protection of the religious institution cannot justify the cover-up of pederasty nor the impunity of those responsible either. No spiritual discourse can legitimize subjugation or stand above people's rights (To be continued).
