Brazil leaves lessons for the Mexican left: security, corruption, young people and new leaderships are key in the face of the regional advance of the far right.

Mexico City, October 5 (SinEmbargo).– The electoral result in Brazil must be read by the Mexican left not as a distant phenomenon nor as an exclusive consequence of United States intervention, but as a warning about problems that can also wear down progressive governments: insecurity, corruption, the lack of renewal of cadres, the distancing of young people and the inability to offer concrete answers to everyday problems, agreed Alejandro Páez Varela, José Hernández and Kurt Hackbarth on VERSUS, a SinEmbargo Al Aire program.

Alejandro Páez Varela said it is necessary to recognize that the political cycle of the so-called Pink Tide in Latin America came to an end and that the return of the right has been accompanied by a leap toward even more radical positions. But, he warned, it would be insufficient to explain that shift solely by Washington's intervention, social media or disinformation campaigns. He said in that sense that there are also responsibilities of their own that the lefts must review.

“It is true that there is manipulation of social media, there is manipulation from the media, but there is also something that failed locally and that the left has to learn to correct and has to correct according to the particularities,” said Páez Varela. “I believe that at the same time common denominators can be found that make it possible to identify structural failures in the nations that turned toward the far right, and those help, in general, to know what we did wrong as a region.”

Páez Varela indicated that one of those failures lies in security, since he said that progressive governments cannot allow crime and violence to become a terrain that is later capitalized on by the right through authoritarian proposals.

“We do have to respond to the populations on the issue of security quickly, soon. That is, there's no ‘let's see if this works,’ no, let's not experiment, we have to go after the thugs, period, now. At the same time we have to work on the causes that generate violence, that is, we have to make a combination of factors to attack the problem of insecurity across the whole continent.”

The journalist indicated that the second warning lies in corruption. On this issue, he maintained, the right has learned to politically exploit the cases that involve progressive governments, while the lefts themselves have been unable to act with the same forcefulness when questioned figures appear in their ranks.

“The right is very corrupt, but the left is also corrupt. It is also corrupt and the difference is that the right knows very well how to amplify the left's corruption cases, while the left doesn't know what to do with the corrupt,” he stated. And regarding the accusations directed against Andrés Manuel López Beltrán, he asked: “Why doesn't he come out and say: ‘Listen, you know what? That is a lie, this and that’? I think that answer must come from him.”

José Hernández agreed that Mexico still retains conditions different from those of other Latin American countries, among them a more politicized society and a history of social mobilization that, in his opinion, have so far hindered a similar growth of the far right. However, he warned that this advantage is not permanent.

“We cannot be complacent about it and that is why it is extremely important to maintain that awareness. And to achieve it, if what the 4T needs is to keep those corrupt opportunists at bay and not allow more of them to arrive nor those who are there to keep growing, but quite the contrary, it would be to eliminate them,” Hernández explained.

Kurt Hackbarth placed another of the great Brazilian lessons in the disenchantment of the new generations. Job precarity, the climate crisis, wars and the perception that the future will be worse have generated, he said, an “anxiety” that can turn into nihilism and that is being capitalized on by the far right.

“Any left movement that needs to combat this has to take that into account; it is not enough to work the margins of the thing. If the social programs are very good, but I still feel that I could lose my job at any moment, it is not enough,” said Hackbarth, who gave as an example the functioning of the public prosecutors' offices, insecurity and citizens' everyday relationship with deficient institutions.

Hernández, for his part, argued that no transformation can rest permanently on a single figure. In contrast to the Latin American projects built around leaderships that seek to prolong themselves in power, he recalled that López Obrador retired from the Presidency under the premise that no one should consider themselves indispensable. The challenge, he said, is to build new cadres capable of keeping the project alive.

“Where are the new cadres and leaderships?” Hernández asked. And he recalled an idea contained in López Obrador's new book: “he prefers to lose rather than present popular or crooked candidates,” a stance he summed up in the maxim that it is preferable “to lose doing the right thing than to win by making mistakes.”
