This article by Josafat Hernández originally appeared in the October 7, 2026 edition of Contralínea, an investigative Mexican news outlet.

So far in 2026, trade between the United States and Mexico has reached record levels. In August of this year, U.S. imports from Mexico reached 60,637.4 million dollars, as we can see in Table 1.

It is the fourth consecutive month in which these imports reach a new high. And this is happening even though Donald Trump's second government has pushed a renegotiation of the Mexico-United States-Canada Agreement full of uncertainty, with threats of tariffs, their imposition on various products and other protectionist policies that seek to promote so-called economic nationalism and the reindustrialization of that country.

However, from January to August 2026, U.S. exports to Mexico and its imports from Mexico have shown a general upward trend, although with declines in some months. And, with this, curiously, the U.S. trade deficit with Mexico that Trump wanted to correct has deepened.

At first glance, these seem like contradictory trends: the more tariffs and the more uncertainty Trump generates regarding the institutional framework of bilateral trade, the more exchange between Mexico and the United States is reinforced. Mexico, in this context, consolidates itself as the main trading partner of the United States and now represents close to 17 percent of that country's total imports, when in 2025 it represented 15 percent. Meanwhile, Canada and China now represent 11 and 7 percent, respectively.

With this, the strategic weight of the Mexican economy in the U.S. economy increases. And it reminds us, once again, that the economic interdependence of both countries obliges us to be good neighbors, without interference or subordination.

However, it is worth bearing in mind that, in the context of the trade war between the United States and China, a process of decoupling between both countries has taken place that has been explained, above all, by national security factors. Hence the talk of securityshoring. Canada, for its part, has entered a phase of trade war with the United States, and that explains why there is also a trend toward greater decoupling between both economies.

These facts must also be interpreted in light of the negotiations that have taken place between Mexico and the United States. The differential between the tariffs that the United States has imposed on Mexico and those it applies to the rest of the world makes it more profitable to keep relocating foreign direct investment (FDI) in a stable economy, with controlled inflation, without an increase in public debt and with a domestic market increasingly strengthened by the expansion of aggregate domestic demand.

Added to this is the capacity to increase quality productive forces through Plan México and the active industrial policy promoted by the current government. In this sense, it is understandable that Mexico has record levels of FDI, which continues to take advantage of the comparative and competitive advantages of the Mexican economy to produce goods aimed at the U.S. market, above all in the electronic components and metallurgy sectors. And this may be due to the expansion of the electronics and computing sector that is occurring in the United States.

And, on the other hand, these are industries that have not yet relocated to the U.S. economy because, for transnational industrial capital, it is not profitable to take on the labor costs and those related to fixed capital to set up in that economy.

Rather, transnational industrial capital is relocating to Mexico, and it will continue to do so as long as producing here is more profitable for it and allows it to obtain a higher rate of profit. This makes it clear that the U.S. economy is not yet in a process of reindustrialization. Rather, what we are seeing is a new international division of labor driven, precisely, by Trump's imposition of tariffs on much of the world.

And this phenomenon of post-neoliberal international trade is, little by little, beginning to take more shape. The protectionist geopolitics of Trumpism, paradoxically, ends up accelerating regional integration with Mexico. And, of course, the ties of mutual interdependence increase.
