This article by Jessica Xantomila originally appeared in the October 20, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left wing daily newspaper.

In publicly presenting its request that Mexico's situation regarding disappeared persons be analyzed by the plenary of the United Nations (UN) General Assembly, the Committee on Enforced Disappearances (CED) said its decision has already produced “a first result,” since, according to the Mexican State, so far in 2026 16,000 disappeared persons have been located, out of the more than 130,000 on record, according to official figures.

“We congratulate these advances,” acknowledged the president of the CED, Ecuadorian Juan Pablo Albán Alencastro, in the framework of his annual report to the Third Committee of the UN General Assembly.

He asked that the cooperation processes Mexico reported in today's session, with countries such as Spain and Germany, as well as with international organizations, “be maintained over time, and that, likewise, those efforts also be maintained with the Committee.”

The CED, he emphasized, has the responsibility to continue monitoring the situation in Mexico, request information, and invite the country to constructive dialogues, as well as continue registering urgent actions.

Position of the Mexican State Before the UN General Assembly

Before the Third Committee of the UN General Assembly, the State for its part reiterated its rejection of the country's situation being taken to the plenary of this highest deliberative body, as requested by the Committee after activating Article 34 of the aforementioned Convention.

“Mexico rejects the interpretation of the Committee on Enforced Disappearances regarding the scope and application of Article 34 of the Convention,” said the Undersecretary for Human Rights of the Secretariat of Governance, Arturo Medina, while emphasizing that the country maintains a policy of openness, dialogue, and international cooperation.

He stressed that “the Mexican State does not consent to or tolerate the disappearance of persons, whatever its modality.” The search for them, he maintained, “does not stop,” and “the pain of the searching mothers and families” is recognized. “Addressing this crime is a national priority.”

International Alliances and Human Identification

He underlined that Mexico has broad collaboration with organizations and other allies. He noted that, with the assistance of the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, it formed a group of international experts to carry out a scientific review of the National Registry of Disappeared and Missing Persons.

He also highlighted the cooperation with countries such as Germany, Spain, Norway, Canada, and Guatemala to strengthen human identification, and the collaboration with the United Nations Population Fund and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights to strengthen search, human identification, and investigation capacities.

He reiterated that the National Strategy for the Search for Disappeared Persons has made it possible to locate more than 16,000 people during 2026.

Medina said this before the delegations of the UN member states present at the session.

Committee Assessments on Crimes Against Humanity and Article 34

Earlier, the president of the CED reiterated that after examining the information provided by the Mexican State, the contributions of victims and civil organizations, as well as the background gathered since 2012, the Committee concluded that “there are well-founded indications of the commission of enforced disappearances as crimes against humanity and of the persistence of that practice to the present day” in the country.

On that basis, he said, it was decided to bring it to the attention of the General Assembly on an urgent basis. However, he emphasized that the decision does not constitute an international responsibility of the Mexican State.

He admitted that there are also no well-founded indications of the persistence of a federal-level policy aimed at the commission of enforced disappearances through deliberate action or omission by the authorities.

“We recognized the efforts made by the Mexican authorities without ignoring the persistence of structural problems that require attention,” Albán said.

He explained that Article 34 is an exceptional preventive alert mechanism designed to mobilize international attention and cooperation in particularly serious situations.

“It is now up to this General Assembly to consider the opportunities for mobilization, international cooperation, and support that the procedure offers to assist, if Mexico considers it necessary, in matters of search, investigation, clarification of the truth, the forensic crisis, and protection of victims and their families.”
