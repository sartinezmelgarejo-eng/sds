This article by Mario Campa originally appeared in the October 28, 2026 edition of SinEmbargo, an independent Mexican digital news outlet.

Mexico is a country generous with foreigners. Its ties of cooperation and solidarity attest to a foreign policy generally on the right side of history. In its time, Mexico took in refugees from Europe who were victims of fascist persecution. Today, the Mexican people are clear about their sympathies. In the asymmetric Israel-Palestine conflict, in reality a genocide according to the consensus of scholars on the subject, Mexico embraces the weak and repudiates the oppressor.

Polls capture a growing repudiation of Washington and Tel Aviv. The latest Latinobarómetro, a series of public opinion surveys associated with the World Values Survey, is emphatic. Mexico is the country in the region most opposed to the war of the United States and Israel against Iran. Eighty-nine percent of respondents express disagreement, a figure well above the regional average of 77 percent. Mexicans' high disapproval of Trump contributes to the mood of rejecting anything that smells of alliance. Netanyahu, close to the White House and protected by Washington, has earned a pariah's reputation. Today, only three out of 10 Latin Americans have a favorable opinion of Israel, an acceptance that falls even further in Mexico.

Rejection of Israel is growing so much that it is already spreading to other areas of public interest. In the recent demonstrations against contaminated water in Guadalajara, Israel's role gained relevance due to citizen outrage over fears of a proposal to privatize the resource and place it under foreign control. Collectives and activists denounced that the technological cooperation agreements and official visits between the Lemus government and Israeli institutions, such as the national water company Mekorot, are in truth a strategy to hand over the management of aquifers to transnational interests. Although the authorities deny any privatization plans, the slogans against Israeli interference energize the demands of the genuine citizen movement.

Far from being an isolated case, mobilizations against Israeli companies are gaining strength in other states. Under the slogan “Israel Out of Yucatán,” hundreds of people marched through the streets of Mérida's Historic Center to protest announcements of investment by Israeli companies in the state. The march brought together citizens of various ages and representatives of Maya communities, who expressed their rejection of agreements related to the management of water resources, agriculture, and security. The protesters cited the international accusations of war crimes against the Israeli government as a cause of their discontent.

Other modest displays of rejection of Israel are sprouting like flowers in spring. In early September 2026, the Coordinating Committee of Mexican Unions for Palestine formally demanded that the Mexican State break all political, economic, and military relations with Israel. On Thursday, October 22, the documentary NAZA, which starts from the hypothesis that the euphemistic “collateral damage” of the Israeli army (IDF) is intentional, premiered in Mexico with initial commercial success, while countries such as Argentina and Costa Rica obstructed its mass distribution. Even the search for an opening act for Ed Sheeran's concert in Mexico was boycotted by popular rage.

Today, being against Israel requires embracing the Palestinian cause. During the 81st UN General Assembly in September 2026, the Mexican government explicitly reaffirmed Mexico's recognition of the State of Palestine. Nevertheless, critical voices demand a tougher official stance against Israel. On a recent visit, British lawmaker Jeremy Corbyn called on the Mexican government to support the International Criminal Court (ICC) arrest warrants against Netanyahu and to cut off trade with Israel as a sanction for the genocide in Gaza. On the other side of the coin, perhaps so as not to irritate Trump, the Mexican government has fallen short in promoting Palestine at book fairs, theaters, schools, and other cultural spaces.

Mexico is not the Latin American country closest to Palestine. That is Chile, for historical reasons. Some half a million descendants make Chilean-Palestinians a dike against the powerful Israeli lobby that wins contracts and manipulates elections in other countries of the continent. Setting aside that atypical case, it is not necessary to have a large foreign community to show affection. Just as it did with the Spanish Republicans persecuted by Franco, Mexico could open its borders further to the influx of Palestinian culture and representation, whose long-term existence is in danger as long as Netanyahu lives. History with a capital H takes note.

Mexico has had enough of ties with Israel. If the so-called “Mexican humanism,” christened by López Obrador, seeks to gain substance, it must embrace a genuine plurality by containing interests linked to Tel Aviv. The Cerimedo case suggests there is much to unravel. That Netanyahu describes the far-right wave sweeping through Latin America, functional to his legitimacy and to Israeli soft power, as “something extraordinary” is a wake-up call. Because of its size and regional influence, Mexico is key in the protest against genocide. Aware of this, many are speaking with their feet and not just with their fingers. With quickened step, the people are walking ahead with gestures of indignation that governments with popular roots are forbidden to ignore.
