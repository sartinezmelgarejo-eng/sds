This editorial originally appeared in the November 1, 2026 edition of La Jornada, Mexico's premier left-wing daily newspaper.

The massive youth-led protests continued yesterday in Europe, with France now stringing together three weekends of demonstrations against the poor state of public education, the high cost of living and the dismantling of the well-being state. Although smaller than on French soil, the demonstrations in Germany stood out for adding opposition to the return of compulsory military service to the common grievances.

The rejection of conscription puts the spotlight on an aspect rarely mentioned by the media when explaining the causes of the European crisis: the immense cost that the continent's societies pay for the proxy war against Russia on Ukrainian territory. In their zeal to destroy Russia, European leaders are destroying their own countries by various routes: they deprive their exports of a key customer, force themselves to buy hydrocarbons at much higher prices, divert resources to the war effort, and cut funding for their citizens' well-being and for strategic sectors such as education.

So far, Germany has handed around 100 billion euros to Kyiv, of which 57 billion correspond to military aid. France has given it almost 8 billion euros bilaterally, and almost 27 billion if what has been channeled through the European Union (EU) is added. Italy, where massive protests have also arisen, contributed 15 billion euros to Volodymyr Zelensky's government. The United Kingdom, whose recent leaders have been notoriously more occupied with their old geopolitical rivalry against Moscow than with addressing the deterioration of British people's quality of life, has put up 25 billion pounds; 16 billion of them for military objectives alone. Other nations have contributed smaller amounts that are nonetheless very significant for their size: Estonia, for example, has already squandered 3 percent of its gross domestic product (GDP).

In addition to the flow of weapons and cash to Kyiv, Europe has embarked on a senseless race to increase its war budget: compared with 2021, the Netherlands allocates 95 percent more to "defense"; Germany, 88 percent more; Italy, 38 percent; France, 17 percent; the United Kingdom, 14 percent. The Baltic nations are the most extreme cases, with a 173 percent increase in Lithuania. All this amid a growing fiscal deficit even in Germany, a hawk of austerity when it comes to financing the population's needs.

While in 2021 Germany paid 25 euros per megawatt-hour of gas, in July of this year it was buying it at almost 50 euros, and in 2022 it came to pay 86 euros for the fuel. In the first quarter of 2025, the EU paid an average of 1.08 euros per cubic meter of U.S. liquefied gas, compared with 0.51 euros for Russian liquefied gas and about 0.32 euros for Russian pipeline gas. That is, by sanctioning Russian hydrocarbons they sanctioned themselves to pay more than triple for U.S. gas, to the delight of Donald Trump and that country's energy companies. According to the Leibniz Institute for Economic Research at the University of Munich (Ifo Institute), between 2021 and 2023 alone Germany lost three points of its GDP due to the energy cost overrun.

After the resounding electoral defeat of his party in last September's regional elections, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said that giving up is not an option for him in the face of the rise of the far right. However, his actions cancel out his words: by deepening neoliberal measures that harm the working class and widen the inequality gap, he serves the country on a platter to the neo-Nazis who offer hatred as a false way out of the abandonment by traditional politicians.
