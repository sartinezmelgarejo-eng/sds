This article by Denisse Torres Hernández originally appeared in the November 26, 2026 edition of SinEmbargo, an independent Mexican digital news outlet.

Sheinbaum defended Mexico's results against organized crime and asked what the U.S. government is doing to stop the distribution of drugs and money laundering on its own territory.

Mexico City, November 26 (SinEmbargo).- President Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo (CSP) criticized on Thursday that the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) is enabling telephone lines to receive reports from Mexico about alleged links of officials and associates with the Sinaloa Cartel, while the agency has pending investigations into drug trafficking, corruption and money laundering in its own country.

The federal government's Security Cabinet joined her questioning, rejecting any unilateral action by the U.S. agency and warning that the country does not accept interference in its territory.

During her morning press conference, the federal head of state considered that this initiative represents interference in Mexican affairs and raised what would happen if the federal government did the same to receive information about alleged acts of corruption on U.S. territory.

For that reason, she insisted that U.S. authorities should concentrate on investigating what happens within their own borders, instead of enabling mechanisms to receive reports on matters that correspond to Mexican institutions.

"It is evident that it is a matter of interference because... Let's put up the news story. The DEA has a lot to do in its own country, a lot. The first thing it should be doing is investigating corruption in the United States linked to organized crime, or what they call 'terrorist groups,'" she stated.

Security Cabinet Rejects Unilateral Actions by the DEA

In a message on its social media, the Security Cabinet reiterated that Mexico "has institutions empowered to receive complaints, investigate crimes, combat corruption and prevent impunity."

Following the DEA's announcement, it maintained that "each country must assume the responsibilities that correspond to it in the fight against organized crime."

The Mexican government also reaffirmed its willingness to maintain international cooperation, although it established four principles for that relationship.

"We reaffirm our commitment to international cooperation, always under four inalienable principles: full respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, shared and differentiated responsibility, mutual respect and trust, and cooperation without subordination," it insisted.

What Is the DEA Doing Against Fentanyl Trafficking in the U.S.?

As part of her arguments, President Sheinbaum Pardo cited an investigation by the Associated Press (AP) news agency about an operation in which the DEA allowed thousands of fentanyl pills to circulate during an investigation in the U.S.

In her response, she compared that case with the operation known as "Fast and Furious," through which weapons were allowed to enter and circulate into Mexico, and questioned the actions of U.S. authorities in the face of drug trafficking: "Recently a news story came out. Look at how serious this is. It's from AP, a recognized news agency. 'The DEA let thousands of fentanyl pills circulate in a major probe.' This is one of many cases, a fentanyl 'Fast and Furious,' only in the United States. Where is the DEA's action?"

Sheinbaum also put the U.S. drug market on the table and asked who is in charge of distributing the narcotics, as well as where the profits from their illegal sale end up, particularly fentanyl.

"As we have said many times, what, there's no drug trafficking in the United States? Well, that's where the consumption is. Who distributes the drugs? Where does the money from the distribution and sale of illegal fentanyl in the United States end up?" she questioned.

In contrast, she defended her government's actions against criminal organizations, including the dismantling of methamphetamine labs, the seizure of fentanyl pills and the arrests of members of criminal groups. She also highlighted that these operations have involved risks for soldiers, members of the National Guard and agents of the Secretariat of Security and Citizen Protection.

The federal head of state stated that homicides in Mexico have fallen practically 50 percent, recalled the sending of 94 criminals to the United States, and maintained that her administration works daily to reduce violence and help stop drug trafficking into that country.

“One Pill Can Kill,” the DEA warned the public for years. Yet agents let at least 1.4 million fentanyl pills go unseized as they spent months monitoring a trafficking ring across six states, an AP investigation found.

Finally, she insisted that bilateral cooperation must include actions on both sides of the border and not be limited to U.S. authorities receiving information about what happens in Mexico.

"Our relevant and reciprocal question is what is the United States doing in the United States, because now it turns out that it has a phone line so that Mexicans can report to the DEA in the United States something that happens in Mexico. Here there is an Attorney General's Office, here the Secretariat of Security works. So they should pay a lot of attention on the other side of the border."
